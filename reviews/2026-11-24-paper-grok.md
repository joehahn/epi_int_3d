   (2) Plain-language physics of each derivation. The paper is harder than this summary where noted.

   - Section 2. An oblate planet pulls a particle back toward the ring plane at frequency \(\nu_a\), almost independently of the in-plane epicycle, because \(\Phi_p\) is even in \(z\) and the tilt is small. The drift stores that bobbing as an inclination and a node, advances the node at \(\Omega_a - \nu_a\), and converts back; the map is exactly invertible even though it drops the \({\cal O}(ei)\) pieces of the true motion. Clear, except the frequency-identity sentence in (1).
   - Section 3 and Appendix A. The ring is narrow, so a nearby streamline looks like a straight wire, and the pull depends only on the perpendicular from the particle to that wire. The code finds the perpendicular by walking from the longitude crossing along the local slope until the remaining vector is orthogonal to the wire. The geometry is “drop the component along the wire”; the algebra of \(c\) and \(N\) is heavier than that.
   - Appendix B. The previous code used the radial gap instead of the perpendicular, took the wire’s direction from the particles’ velocity rather than the shape, and used the wrong streamline’s linear density. Only the direction matters for waves: near an \(m\)-armed resonance the shape is about \(m\) times steeper than the velocity, so the old tangential pull, which is what moves angular momentum between streamlines, was low by about \(m\). Harder than this, especially the Hahn & Spitale (2013) paragraph.
   - Section 4.1 and Appendix D. A tilted satellite’s vertical tug is a set of rotating \(m\)-armed patterns. Each resonates where a ring particle sees it at frequency \(\nu_a\). Self-gravity of a bent sheet pulls the sheet back toward the plane, so a trailing wave can carry the disturbance away from the resonance, inward at an inner vertical resonance. The forcing-phase step and the torque paragraph are the parts that are harder, or misleading, relative to this.
   - Section 4.2. Same satellite, now in the plane, so it pushes radially and azimuthally. Self-gravity weakens the in-plane restoring force, and the wave travels outward from an inner Lindblad resonance. This section is the easy one: it only lists what flips.
   - Appendix C. Discrete wires underestimate the gravity of the shortest ripple the grid can hold, and the pressure stencil misses that ripple entirely, so a cold lattice stays stable past the continuum Toomre limit if the wires are not too close and too massive. Viscosity tightens that limit, because the same short ripples can grow when self-gravity helps the stress. Separately, the longitude interpolation can seed a sawtooth around each streamline that also grows with gravity off. The overstability formula and the “220, 170 and 850 orbits” sentence are harder than this; Fig. 9 is the clear part.
   - Appendix E. A steady wavetrain does not fade as \(\sigma \rightarrow 0\). It gets taller as \(\sigma^{-1/2}\), because the torque and the group velocity both shrink with \(\sigma\), and the angular momentum per unit area stays finite. At any finite time the wave has not gotten far, so a massless ring shows only the forced bobbing. This appendix is clearer than the main text’s summary of it.

## Strongest parts

The vertical drift is the right extension of Hahn et al. (2025): Eqns. (11) and (13) are exact inverses, the nodal rate matches \(\Omega_a - \nu_a\), and the rotation form matches V8. Appendix A’s wire acceleration matches G11/G16, including \(\Delta^2 = d_r^2 + d_z^2 - (Nc)^2\), and the planar reduction to Eqns. (A1) is correct. The bending- and density-wave ODE solutions, amplitudes, wavelengths and group velocities match B8, B15, B19, L5–L7, including the sign flip that sends one wave in and the other out. Appendix E is the best piece of writing in the draft: the integration by parts is right, and the \(\sigma^{-1/2}\) steady wave versus the finite-time massless limit is the correct resolution of that paradox. The wave-test writeups do what P30 and P31 ask: amplitude and torque are numbers, the wavenumber excess is not hidden, and the free oscillation, the outer-edge forced response, and the edge eccentricity are named. The \({\cal R}\) arithmetic in Appendix C (0.09, 0.3, 0.9, the taper, \(X(\pi)\) half the continuum, \(2/7\) and \(4/5\)) checks out.

## Weakest parts

The torque paragraph in Section 4.1.4 says the satellite exerts a torque the kick does not apply, and Fig. 6 is then read as if it tested delivery of that torque. The main text and the abstract flatten Appendix E into “\(h \rightarrow h_{\rm forced}\) as \(\sigma \rightarrow 0\)”. Appendix C finds a numerical instability in the same interpolation the wave runs use, leaves it open, and does not tell Section 4. Several quantitative claims (the \(10^{-7}\) and \(10^{-4}\) corrections, the \(J_2\)–self-gravity scaling, the cause of the wavenumber excess) are ahead of what is shown, even when the notes already contain the sharper test. The comparison to “Keplerian” formulas is not tied to the \(J_2\) actually integrated.

## What a skeptical referee would push back on

The referee would accept the vertical map and the wire formula, and would not rerun the Fresnel algebra. The pushback would be on whether the Section 4 tests measure what they claim. First: if the bending-wave kick is only \(A_z\), then \(T/T_V\) is an amplitude-and-phase diagnostic, not a torque, and the angular-momentum sentence has to be rewritten before the conservation figure means anything. Second: an open \(m \simeq N_t/2\) instability in the force interpolation has to be shown to be absent in the wave runs, or those runs are not a clean test of Shu (1984). Third: the residuals called “of order \(x\)” and “because the ring is a finite lattice” need the \(J_2\)-consistent \({\cal D}\) and the B13 curve, both of which already exist, before “excellent agreement” and the lattice explanation can stand. Fourth: Eqn. (40)’s minus sign needs a cited equation and a statement of what it does to \(L_z\). The placeholder introduction would be sent back, but that is draft status, not a scientific objection.
