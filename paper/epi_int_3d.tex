\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

%placeholder
The \code{epi\_int} and \code{epi\_int\_lite} N-body integrators \citep{HS13, H25} model a planetary ring as
a set of gravitating streamlines whose particles are confined to the ring plane. Spiral bending
waves, which are launched at a satellite's vertical resonances \citep{SCL83, S84}, require the
particles' vertical motions too. This paper describes \code{epi\_int\_3d}, a modest extension of
\code{epi\_int\_lite} into the vertical (out-of-plane) direction, and uses it to simulate spiral
bending waves.


\section{Vertical epicyclic motion}
\label{sec:vertical}

The \code{epi\_int\_lite} drift step advances each particle's unperturbed orbit about an oblate planet
using first-order epicyclic equations that are exactly reversible: converting spatial
coordinates into epicyclic orbit elements and back introduces no error, so the drift step
is not a source of numerical drift \citep{H25}. Here those equations are extended to
particles having vertical displacement $z$
and vertical velocity $v_z$, with the same first-order accuracy and the same exact
reversibility.

\subsection{Equations of motion}
\label{sec:eom}

A particle orbiting an oblate planet obeys Newton's second law,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:newton}
    \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\nabla\Phi_p,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi_p$ is the planet's gravitational potential. That potential is a series in the
planet's zonal harmonics $J_{2k}$ (Eqn.\ 5 of \citealt{BL94}), and as in \cite{HS13} and
\cite{H25} only the $J_2$ term is kept, so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:Phi3d}
    \Phi_p(r,z) = -\frac{GM_p}{\rho}\left[1 - J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{\rho}\right)^2P_2\left(\frac{z}{\rho}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Here $M_p$ is the planet's mass, $R_p$ its radius, $r$ the particle's cylindrical radius,
$\rho = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}$ the particle's distance from the planet's center, and
$P_2(z/\rho) = (3z^2/\rho^2 - 1)/2$ is the second Legendre polynomial. In cylindrical coordinates
$(r, \theta, z)$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:newton}) is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:eom_cyl}
\begin{eqnarray}
    \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi_p}{\partial r} \label{eqn:eom_cyl_a}\\
    \frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\dot{\theta}\right) &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi_p}{\partial\theta} = 0 \label{eqn:eom_cyl_b}\\
    \ddot{z} &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi_p}{\partial z}. \label{eqn:eom_cyl_c}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
The potential is independent of $\theta$, so the $z$-component of the specific angular
momentum, $h_z = r^2\dot{\theta} = rv_\theta$, is conserved exactly.
Because the potential is even in $z$, near the ring plane
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:Phi_expand}
    \Phi_p(r,z) \simeq \Phi_p(r,0) + \frac{1}{2}\nu^2(r)z^2,
\end{equation}
where terms of order $z^4$ are neglected, $\Phi_p(r,0) = -GM_p/r - J_2GM_pR_p^2/2r^3$ is the midplane potential and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:nu_r}
    \nu^2(r) = \left.\frac{\partial^2\Phi_p}{\partial z^2}\right|_{z=0}
        = \frac{GM_p}{r^3}\left[1 + \frac{9}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{r}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}
Equations (\ref{eqn:eom_cyl}) then become
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:eom_expand}
\begin{eqnarray}
    \ddot{r} &\simeq& \frac{h_z^2}{r^3} - \left.\frac{\partial\Phi_p}{\partial r}\right|_{z=0} \label{eqn:eom_r}\\
    \ddot{z} &\simeq& -\nu^2(r)z, \label{eqn:eom_z}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
which neglect terms of order $z^2$ in the radial equation and $z^3$ in the vertical one.

So an unperturbed particle's horizontal and vertical motions are decoupled to first order in its small
departures from a circular orbit in the ring plane. The ring's internal forces, its self-gravity,
viscosity and pressure, can re-couple them. The vertical equation is that of a harmonic oscillator,
so $\nu(r)$ is the frequency at which a particle at radius $r$ oscillates vertically.

\subsection{Unperturbed motion}
\label{sec:elem2coords}

\cite{H25} solved the planar equation of motion, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:eom_r}), to first
order in small displacements from circular motion, taking care to make the transformation from the
coordinates $r, \theta, v_r, v_\theta$ to the epicyclic elements $a, e, \tilde{\omega}, M$ exactly
reversible, so that it can be used in a drift-kick N-body integrator like the one used here. That
solution (Appendix A of \citealt{H25}) is summarized here and then extended to $|z| > 0$.

The particle's coordinates are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:inplane}
\begin{eqnarray}
    r &=& \frac{a}{\sqrt{1 + 2e\cos M}} \label{eqn:inplane_a}\\
    \theta &=& \frac{\Omega_a}{\kappa_a}(M + 2e\sin M) + \tilde{\omega} \label{eqn:inplane_b}\\
    v_r &=& ea\kappa_a\sin M\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^3 \label{eqn:inplane_c}\\
    v_\theta &=& \frac{a^2\Omega_a}{r}, \label{eqn:inplane_d}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
when written in terms of the epicyclic elements (Eqn.\ A9 of \citealt{H25}), where the mean angular
velocity and epicyclic frequency at semimajor axis $a$ are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:Omega_kappa}
\begin{eqnarray}
    \Omega_a &=& \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 + \frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2} \label{eqn:Omega_kappa_a}\\
    \kappa_a &=& \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 - \frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2}. \label{eqn:Omega_kappa_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
Equations (\ref{eqn:inplane}) conserve the vertical component of the particle's specific
angular momentum vector, $h_z = rv_\theta = a^2\Omega_a$, and also solve the planar equation of motion
with errors of order ${\cal O}(e^2)$. The particle's cylindrical radius, the length of its position
vector projected onto the ring plane, is $r = a[1 + {\cal O}(e)]$. Its mean anomaly advances with time $t$
at the epicyclic frequency, $M = \kappa_a t + M_0$, while $a$, $e$ and $\tilde{\omega}$ are constant.

Equation (\ref{eqn:eom_z}) is already first order in the small quantities, so $\nu(r) \simeq \nu(a)$
and the vertical oscillation frequency becomes
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:nu}
    \nu_a \equiv \nu(a) = \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 + \frac{9}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
(Eqn.\ 17 of \citealt{BL94}), where the neglected terms are of order ${\cal O}(ei)$ and ${\cal O}(i^3)$. Outside the planet its potential
obeys Laplace's equation, which for an axisymmetric
potential is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:laplace}
    \nabla^2\Phi_p = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\Phi_p}{\partial r}\right)
        + \frac{\partial^2\Phi_p}{\partial z^2} = 0.
\end{equation}
Note that $\Omega_a^2 = r^{-1}\partial\Phi_p/\partial r$,
$\kappa_a^2 = 3\Omega_a^2 + \partial^2\Phi_p/\partial r^2$ and
$\nu_a^2 = \partial^2\Phi_p/\partial z^2$, so Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:laplace}) relates the three
frequencies via $\kappa_a^2 + \nu_a^2 = 2\Omega_a^2$.

The solution of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:eom_z}) with $\nu = \nu_a$ is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:zvz}
\begin{eqnarray}
    z   &=& a\,i\sin(\theta - \Omega) \label{eqn:zvz_a}\\
    v_z &=& a\nu_a i\cos(\theta - \Omega), \label{eqn:zvz_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
whose amplitude and phase define two new orbit elements, the inclination $i$ and the longitude of
ascending node $\Omega$. The node regresses at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:node}
    \frac{d\Omega}{dt} = \Omega_a - \nu_a \simeq -\frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\Omega_a.
\end{equation}
Also keep in mind that $\Omega$ is the longitude of ascending node, while $\Omega_a$ is the mean angular
velocity. Equations (\ref{eqn:zvz}) neglect terms of order ${\cal O}(ei)$, as Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:nu}) does.

\subsection{Coordinates to elements}
\label{sec:coords2elem}

Equations (\ref{eqn:inplane}) and (\ref{eqn:zvz}) can be inverted exactly via five steps, with the
first four from Appendix A of \cite{H25}:
\begin{enumerate}\raggedright
\item Compute $h_z = rv_\theta$, then solve $h_z^2 = a^4\Omega_a^2$ for the semimajor axis,
    $a = R_p\left(C + \sqrt{C^2 - 3J_2/2}\right)$ where $C = h_z^2/2GM_pR_p$.
\item Compute $\Omega_a$ and $\kappa_a$ from Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:Omega_kappa}).
\item Compute $e\sin M = (v_r/a\kappa_a)(a/r)^3$ and $e\cos M = \frac{1}{2}[(a/r)^2 - 1]$,
    which give $e = \sqrt{(e\sin M)^2 + (e\cos M)^2}$ and $M = \text{atan2}(e\sin M, e\cos M)$.
\item Compute $\tilde{\omega} = \theta - (\Omega_a/\kappa_a)(M + 2e\sin M)$.
\item The new fifth step: compute $\nu_a$ from Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:nu}), then $i$ and $\Omega$ from
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:i_node}
\begin{eqnarray}
    i &=& \sqrt{\left(\frac{z}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{v_z}{a\nu_a}\right)^2} \label{eqn:i_node_a}\\
    \theta - \Omega &=& \text{atan2}(z, v_z/\nu_a). \label{eqn:i_node_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
\end{enumerate}
Once $a$ is known, the mapping $(i, \theta - \Omega) \leftrightarrow (z, v_z/\nu_a)$ is a
change between polar and Cartesian coordinates, so Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) and
(\ref{eqn:i_node}) are exact inverses.


\section{Ring forces}
\label{sec:gravity}

A ring particle's epicyclic motion is also perturbed by the accelerations that the surrounding ring
exerts on it: the ring's self-gravity, its pressure, and its viscosity, which accounts for the dissipative
collisions that occur as particles in adjacent streamlines shear past one another. \code{Epi\_int\_3d}
computes these accelerations as \code{epi\_int\_lite} does (Section 2 of \citealt{H25}). Its particles
are assigned to streamlines, each a closed eccentric path around the planet that is populated by
particles that share a common semimajor axis $a$ and are distributed in longitude, so the particles
trace out their streamlines. The particles do not interact with each other directly. Rather, each
particle is perturbed by the accelerations exerted by the other streamlines, whose positions, slopes
and linear densities near the particle are inferred from the particles that trace them.

\subsection{Gravity}
\label{sec:ring_gravity}

%equations G1-G19, sympy-checked in notes/sympy/streamline_gravity.py; figures by figures/wire_planar.py
All \code{epi\_int\_3d} simulations span only a narrow annulus within a broader planetary ring, so every particle is
radially close to the nearby portions of all perturbing streamlines. Which is why \code{epi\_int\_3d} and its
predecessors (\code{epi\_int} and \code{epi\_int\_lite}) assume that the gravitational acceleration of a particle by the particles inhabiting the perturbing
streamline at semimajor axis $a'$ is that due to a straight and infinitely long wire:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_Ag}
    \mathbf{A}_g = \frac{2G\lambda'}{\Delta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda'$ is the perturbing streamline's linear density, $\Delta$ is the particle's distance
from the streamline's nearest point, which is labeled the near point in Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar},
and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = \boldsymbol{\Delta}/\Delta$ is the unit vector that points from the
particle toward the near point.

\subsubsection{The perturbing streamline's geometry}
\label{sec:wire}

The following 3D treatment is an extension of the 2D force calculations used in \cite{HS13}.
In cylindrical coordinates the particle is at $\mathbf{r} = (r, \theta, z)$. The ring matter
that is represented by the particles in perturbing streamline $a'$ is at
$\mathbf{r}' = (r', \theta', z')$ that traces a continuous wire, so these coordinates are
to be regarded as functions of longitude $\theta'$, i.e.,
$\mathbf{r}' = (r'(\theta'), \theta', z'(\theta'))$. Lagrange polynomials will be fit to the particles
in streamline $a'$, which means that $r'(\theta')$, $z'(\theta')$ and the streamline's linear density
$\lambda'(\theta')$ can be regarded as known functions in the following. And because the particle is close to the streamline, $|r' - r| \ll r$,
$|z' - z| \ll r$ and $|\theta' - \theta| \ll 1$, so the particle's local frame
$(\hat{\mathbf{r}}, \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}, \hat{\mathbf{z}})$ can be treated as Cartesian.

The vector from the particle to the streamline's near point (the black square in
Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}) can be written
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_d}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} = \mathbf{r}'(\theta') - \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{d}_0 + \mathbf{d}_1,
\end{equation}
where $\theta' = \theta + \delta\theta'$ is the near point's longitude as measured in the $z = 0$
plane, and $\delta\theta'$ is its longitude relative to the particle's with $|\delta\theta'| \ll 1$. This splits
the trip to the near point across two legs. The first leg, $\mathbf{d}_0 = \mathbf{r}'(\theta) - \mathbf{r}$,
travels from the particle to the crossing point $\mathbf{r}'(\theta) = (r'(\theta), \theta, z'(\theta))$, which is where the streamline crosses the
particle's longitude (the cross in Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}), so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_d0}
    \mathbf{d}_0 = d_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + d_z\hat{\mathbf{z}},
\end{equation}
where $d_r = r'(\theta) - r$ and $d_z = z'(\theta) - z$. The second leg,
$\mathbf{d}_1 = \boldsymbol{\Delta} - \mathbf{d}_0 = \mathbf{r}'(\theta') - \mathbf{r}'(\theta)$, then runs along the streamline from
the crossing point to the near point. This is a right triangle
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}), so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_pythag}
    |\mathbf{d}_0|^2 = \Delta^2 + |\mathbf{d}_1|^2.
\end{equation}
That stretch of streamline is short and locally straight, so
$\mathbf{d}_1 \simeq [r'(\theta') - r'(\theta)]\hat{\mathbf{r}} + r'(\theta)\,\delta\theta'\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
+ [z'(\theta') - z'(\theta)]\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, which to first order in $\delta\theta'$ is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_d1}
    \mathbf{d}_1 \simeq (s_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + s_z\hat{\mathbf{z}})\,r'\,\delta\theta'
        = N r'\,\delta\theta'\,\hat{\mathbf{t}},
\end{equation}
where $r'$ is short for $r'(\theta)$. This uses $r'(\theta') - r'(\theta) \simeq (dr'/d\theta')\delta\theta'$
and likewise for $z'$, where the streamline's radial and vertical slopes are
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_slopes}
    s_r = \left.\frac{1}{r'}\frac{dr'}{d\theta'}\right|_{\theta' = \theta} \quad \mbox{and} \quad
    s_z = \left.\frac{1}{r'}\frac{dz'}{d\theta'}\right|_{\theta' = \theta},
\end{equation}
which are evaluated at the perturbed particle's longitude $\theta' = \theta$ and are obtained from the
derivatives of the Lagrange polynomials. Together they give $s = (s_r^2 + s_z^2)^{1/2}$, which is the
streamline's slope relative to circular motion in the ring plane, i.e., the
$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ direction, so the streamline is tilted from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ by
the angle $\varphi$ where $\tan\varphi = s$. Note that as $\delta\theta' \rightarrow 0$ the chord $\mathbf{d}_1$ points along the streamline, so $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ is also the perturbing streamline's unit tangent:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_t}
    \hat{\mathbf{t}} = \frac{s_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + s_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}}{N},
\end{equation}
where $N^2 = 1 + s_r^2 + s_z^2$.

The near point is where $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ is perpendicular to the streamline,
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} = (\mathbf{d}_0 + \mathbf{d}_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} = 0$. And because
$\mathbf{d}_1$ lies along $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$, this requires
$\mathbf{d}_1 = -(\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})\hat{\mathbf{t}}$, so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_D}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} = \mathbf{d}_0 + \mathbf{d}_1 = \mathbf{d}_0 - (\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})\hat{\mathbf{t}},
\end{equation}
which is simply $\mathbf{d}_0$ minus its component along the streamline.

Now equate $\mathbf{d}_1 = -(\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ to Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_d1}), which shows
that the near point lies at $\delta\theta' = -c/r'$ where $c = (\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})/N = (d_rs_r + d_zs_z)/N^2$. So at the near point
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_d1near}
    \mathbf{d}_1 = -Nc\,\hat{\mathbf{t}} = -c(s_r\hat{\mathbf{r}} + \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + s_z\hat{\mathbf{z}}).
\end{equation}
Adding $\mathbf{d}_0$ to the above then yields $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:wire_Dcomp}
\begin{eqnarray}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} &=& (d_r - cs_r)\hat{\mathbf{r}} - c\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} + (d_z - cs_z)\hat{\mathbf{z}} \label{eqn:wire_Dcomp_a}\\
    \Delta^2 &=& d_r^2 + d_z^2 - (Nc)^2. \label{eqn:wire_Dcomp_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
Inserting $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ into Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) then provides the acceleration that
streamline $a'$ exerts on the particle:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:wire_Acomp}
\begin{eqnarray}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'(d_r - cs_r)}{\Delta^2} \label{eqn:wire_Acomp_a}\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda'c}{\Delta^2} \label{eqn:wire_Acomp_b}\\
    A_{g,z} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'(d_z - cs_z)}{\Delta^2}, \label{eqn:wire_Acomp_c}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
where $N^2 = 1 + s_r^2 + s_z^2$ and $c = (d_rs_r + d_zs_z)/N^2$.

Lastly, note that because the streamline is tilted from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ by the angle
$\varphi$, the right triangle of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_pythag}) has
$|\mathbf{d}_1| \le \Delta\tan\varphi = s\Delta$, with equality in the ring plane
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}). An $m$-armed disturbance displaces streamline $a'$ radially by about
$ea'$ and vertically by about $ia'$ where $e$ and $i$ are its particles' eccentricity and
inclination, so Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_slopes}) give $s_r \sim me$ and $s_z \sim mi$, and
$s \lesssim m(e^2 + i^2)^{1/2}$. Rings are nearly circular and nearly planar, and $m$ is modest for
the waves of interest, so $s \ll 1$ and $|\mathbf{d}_1| \ll \Delta$, which means that the near point
lies very close to the crossing point.

\subsubsection{Computing the acceleration}
\label{sec:wire_code}

%Phase 3 step 2: coded as fast/kernels.py ring_gravity_3d (notes/phase3/vertical_gravity.md)
%TODO: lambda = m_1 Omega_a/(2 pi v) assumes a stationary streamline; see notes/phase2/streamline_gravity.md
The particles' coordinates are stored in arrays whose rows
are the streamlines, and every timestep, before the accelerations are computed, the particles in each
row are sorted by longitude. Because the streamlines are nearly concentric, the particle in row $a'$
that has the perturbed particle's column index lies at nearly the same longitude, so it and its
neighbor on either side bracket $\theta$.
Lagrange polynomials fit to those three particles then provide, by interpolation to $\theta$, the
crossing point's $r'(\theta)$ and $z'(\theta)$ and hence $d_r$ and $d_z$, the linear density
$\lambda'(\theta)$, and, from the polynomials' derivatives, the slopes $s_r$ and $s_z$ of
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_slopes}), and Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Dcomp})--(\ref{eqn:wire_Acomp}) then provide the
acceleration. This is repeated for every perturbing streamline, and the sum is the particle's acceleration due to
ring gravity. Note that $\lambda'$ is evaluated at the crossing point rather than at the near point,
which is justified since Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) assumes that $\lambda'$ varies insignificantly over
distances of several $\Delta$ along the streamline, while the near point lies only
$|\mathbf{d}_1| \ll \Delta$ from the crossing point.

\subsection{Pressure}
\label{sec:pressure}

\code{Epi\_int\_lite} treats the ring as a dilute gas of colliding particles whose one-dimensional
pressure is $p = c^2\sigma$ where $c$ is the particles' velocity dispersion and $\sigma$ is the ring's
surface density (Section 2.2 of \citealt{H25}). Its treatment in three dimensions, and a test against
the theory of spiral density waves in a non-gravitating, pressure-supported gas disk, will be
completed later.

\subsection{Viscosity}
\label{sec:viscosity}

\code{Epi\_int\_3d} uses the shear and bulk viscosity of \code{epi\_int\_lite} (Section 2.2 of
\citealt{H25}; Appendix A of \citealt{HS13}), which act only in the ring plane. A vertical viscosity,
which would damp bending waves, will be completed later. The bending-wave simulations of
Section \ref{sec:waves} have no viscosity or pressure.

\subsection{Advancing the particles}
\label{sec:integrator}

\code{Epi\_int\_3d} advances the particles with the second-order symplectic kick-drift-kick scheme of
\code{epi\_int\_lite}, which is that of the MERCURY N-body integrator \citep{C99}; see Section 2.2 of
\cite{H25}. Each timestep $\Delta t$ starts with a kick that changes each particle's velocity by the
ring's accelerations, plus any satellite's (Section \ref{sec:waves}), applied over $\Delta t/2$. Then a
drift advances the particle along its unperturbed orbit for $\Delta t$, and a second half kick ends the
timestep. The drift converts coordinates to elements (Section \ref{sec:coords2elem}), advances
$M \rightarrow M + \kappa_a\Delta t$ and $\Omega \rightarrow \Omega + (\Omega_a - \nu_a)\Delta t$, and
converts back, so it is exact for the unperturbed motion of Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:inplane}) and (\ref{eqn:zvz}).
Its vertical part is simply a rotation of $(z, v_z/\nu_a)$ through the angle $\Delta\theta - (\Omega_a - \nu_a)\Delta t$,
where $\Delta\theta$ is the drift's change in the particle's longitude. Before and after each drift, small shifts of the
particles' coordinates account for the planet's motion about the system's center of mass \citep{C99}.


\section{Using spiral wave theory to test \code{epi\_int\_3d}}
\label{sec:waves}

Here we use \code{epi\_int\_3d} to simulate the spiral bending waves that a satellite launches at its
vertical resonances in a planetary ring, which exercises the code's new vertical capability, and we compare
them with linear wave theory to confirm the code. These waves test the vertical motion of
Section \ref{sec:vertical} and the vertical ring gravity of Section \ref{sec:ring_gravity}. We will also
simulate the spiral density waves that a satellite launches at its Lindblad resonances, to further validate
the code's ability to simulate horizontal disturbances in a self-gravitating ring, and in a
non-gravitating, pressure-supported gas disk those waves will also test the ring pressure of
Section \ref{sec:pressure}. Each test's notebook, which makes the comparisons shown here and others, is
in the code's repository; the simulations themselves are not.

\subsection{Spiral bending waves}
\label{sec:bending}

%equations B1-B12 of notes/sympy/bending_wave.py; notes/phase2c/bending_wave_theory.md
\subsubsection{Free spiral bending waves}
\label{sec:waves_free}

A spiral bending wave displaces the ring vertically by $Z = {\rm Re}[h(r)e^{i(m\theta - \omega t)}]$, where the
integer $m$ and the frequency $\omega$ are constant and the complex amplitude $h \propto e^{i\int k\,dr}$ winds
with radial wavenumber $k$. The wave's crests, where $m\theta + \int k\,dr - \omega t$ is constant, rotate with
the pattern speed $\Omega_p = \omega/m$, prograde when $\Omega_p > 0$, and a ring particle orbiting at $\Omega_a$ sees
the wave at the Doppler-shifted frequency $\omega_m = m(\Omega_a - \Omega_p)$. The wave obeys the dispersion
relation (Eqn.\ (84) of \citealt{S84})\footnote{\cite{S84} writes perturbations as $e^{i(\omega t - m\theta)}$,
the complex conjugate of the form used here. His equations are therefore the complex conjugates of those here, with
$k$ of opposite sign: his trailing waves at an inner vertical resonance have $k < 0$, where those here have $k > 0$.}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:bw_disp}
    \omega_m^2 = \nu_a^2 + 2\pi G\sigma|k|,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the ring's surface density, whose self-gravity adds to the vertical restoring force
(Appendix \ref{sec:wire_continuum}). Since $\omega = m\Omega_a - \omega_m$, the wave's group velocity is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:bw_cg}
    c_g = \frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k} = -{\rm sgn}(k)\frac{\pi G\sigma}{\omega_m}
\end{equation}
(Eqn.\ (85) of \citealt{S84}). The dispersion relation fixes only $|k|$, and the group velocity's sign sets the
sign of $k$. A wave is launched at a vertical resonance, where $\omega_m = \nu_a > 0$, and its angular momentum
luminosity, which equals the torque that launches it, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_torque}), flows away from the
resonance, into the region where $\omega_m^2 > \nu_a^2$. At an inner vertical resonance, where $m > 0$, that region
lies inside the resonance, so $c_g < 0$ and $k > 0$; at an outer vertical resonance, where $m < 0$, it lies
outside, so $c_g > 0$ and $k < 0$. Either way $k$ has the sign of $m$, so along a crest the longitude
$\theta$ decreases with $r$, and the wave is trailing \citep{S84}.

\subsubsection{Forced spiral bending waves}
\label{sec:waves_theory}

A satellite of mass $M_s$ whose circular orbit of radius $a_s$ is inclined by $i_s$ to the ring plane
launches spiral bending waves at its vertical resonances \citep{SCL83, S84}. To first order in $i_s$ and $z$, the
satellite accelerates a ring particle at radius $r$ and longitude $\theta$ vertically by
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_full}
    A_z = -\frac{GM_s(z - z_s)}{\Delta_s^3}
\end{equation}
where $z_s = a_si_s\sin(\theta_s - \Omega_s)$ is the satellite's height above the ring plane, its longitude
$\theta_s = \Omega_{a_s}t$ and longitude of ascending node $\Omega_s = (\Omega_{a_s} - \nu_{a_s})t$ advance at
constant rates from zero at time $t = 0$, and $\Delta_s$ is the particle's distance from the satellite, where
$\Delta_s^2 = r^2 + a_s^2 - 2ra_s\cos(\theta - \theta_s)$ omits $(z - z_s)^2$, which is of second order.
The ring's vertical excursions are much smaller than the satellite's, $|z| \ll a_si_s$, so
$A_z \simeq GM_sz_s/\Delta_s^3$, whose Fourier expansion in $\theta$ is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_series}
    A_z = -\sum_{m=-\infty}^\infty A_m\sin[m\theta - (m\Omega_{a_s} + \nu_{a_s})t]
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_AV}
    A_m = \frac{GM_si_s}{2a_s^2}b_{3/2}^{(m)}(\beta)
\end{equation}
in which
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:laplace_b}
    b_{3/2}^{(m)}(\beta) = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\pi\frac{\cos m\phi\,d\phi}{(1 + \beta^2 - 2\beta\cos\phi)^{3/2}}
\end{equation}
is a Laplace coefficient of $\beta = r/a_s$ (Eqn.\ (A21) of \citealt{S84}). Each
term with $|m| \ge 2$ is an $|m|$-armed pattern that rotates with angular velocity
$\Omega_p = \Omega_{a_s} + \nu_{a_s}/m$ and launches a spiral bending wave at its one vertical resonance, which
lies interior to the satellite's orbit when $m > 0$ and exterior when $m < 0$, and these resonances are well
separated across the ring \citep{S84}. The $m = -1$ term drives a secular resonance where the ring's nodes
regress at the satellite's rate, which is not considered here, and the $m = 0$ and $m = 1$ terms have no resonance. The simulations here follow particles near a single $m$th resonance, so
\code{Epi\_int\_3d} keeps only the resonant term of the satellite's forcing,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_force}
    A_z = -A_V\sin m(\theta - \Omega_pt),
\end{equation}
where $A_V$ is $A_m$ evaluated at $\beta = r_V/a_s$. A particle feels the forcing at the Doppler-shifted forcing
frequency $\omega_m$ of Section \ref{sec:waves_free}, and the inner vertical resonance lies where $\omega_m = \nu_a$, at the
radius $r_V \simeq a_s[(m - 1)/(m + 1)]^{2/3}$ when $J_2$ is small. There the quantity
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_D}
    D \equiv \nu_a^2 - \omega_m^2,
\end{equation}
a measure of the distance from resonance in frequency, vanishes.

Near $r_V$ the ring's vertical displacement is $Z = \mathrm{Re}[h(x)e^{im(\theta - \Omega_pt)}]$ where
$x = (r - r_V)/r_V$ is the fractional distance from resonance, and $D \simeq {\cal D}x$ where
${\cal D} = r\,dD/dr$ at $r_V$ (Eqn.\ (87a) of \citealt{S84}), which is $3(m - 1)\Omega_a^2$ when oblateness is
negligible. The dispersion relation, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:bw_disp}), is $D = -2\pi G\sigma|k|$, and the wave
here is trailing, with $k > 0$. Acting on $h \propto e^{i\int k\,dr}$, $k$ is $-i\,d/dr$, which turns the
dispersion relation into the left side of Eqn.\ (88) of \cite{S84},
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_ode}
    -i\epsilon\frac{dh}{dx} + xh = L_V,
\end{equation}
whose right side is the satellite's forcing, where
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_params}
    \epsilon = \frac{2\pi G\sigma}{r_V{\cal D}} \quad \mbox{and} \quad L_V = \frac{iA_V}{\cal D}.
\end{equation}
The solution of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_ode}) that has
no wave at $x > 0$ is (Eqns.\ (89)--(90) of \citealt{S84})
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_h}
    h(x) = -\frac{iL_V}{\epsilon}e^{-ix^2/2\epsilon}\int_x^\infty e^{is^2/2\epsilon}\,ds.
\end{equation}
Far from resonance, where $|x| \gg \epsilon^{1/2}$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_h})
tends to Eqns.\ (91)--(92) of \cite{S84},
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:vres_sol}
\begin{eqnarray}
    h &=& h_{\rm forced} + h_{\rm wave}, \label{eqn:vres_sol_h}\\
    \mbox{where} \quad h_{\rm forced} &=& \frac{L_V}{x}, \label{eqn:vres_sol_forced}\\
    \mbox{and} \quad h_{\rm wave} &=& \left\{\begin{array}{ll} Z_w e^{-ix^2/2\epsilon} & \qquad x \ll -\epsilon^{1/2} \\
        0 & \qquad x \gg \epsilon^{1/2}. \end{array}\right. \label{eqn:vres_sol_wave}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
The forced oscillation $h_{\rm forced}$, which is $iA_V/D$ to first order in $x$, is the response of a massless ring, and $h_{\rm wave}$ is a spiral bending wave. The wave's amplitude
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_amp}
    |Z_w| = \left(\frac{2\pi}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}|L_V| = A_V\left(\frac{r_V}{G\sigma{\cal D}}\right)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
is constant, while its wavelength
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_lambda}
    \lambda_{\rm wave} = \frac{4\pi^2G\sigma}{{\cal D}|x|}
\end{equation}
shrinks with distance from resonance. The wave's first wavelength, from $r_V$ to where its phase
$x^2/2\epsilon$ reaches $2\pi$, is $(4\pi\epsilon)^{1/2}r_V$. Appendix \ref{sec:massless_limit} shows that
$h \rightarrow h_{\rm forced}$ as $\sigma \rightarrow 0$.

Equations (\ref{eqn:vres_ode})--(\ref{eqn:vres_lambda}) give us a few opportunities to test
\code{epi\_int\_3d}'s vertical motion. The forced oscillation alone is tested by a ring of massless
particles (Section \ref{sec:waves_massless}), which checks the forcing $A_V$, the resonance's location
$r_V$ and ${\cal D}$, which together also set the wave's amplitude. The wave is tested by a
self-gravitating ring (Section \ref{sec:waves_ring}). A third test may include the ring's viscosity,
which damps the wave \citep[Eqns.\ (97)--(98) of][]{S84}.

\subsubsection{Massless particles}
\label{sec:waves_massless}

A ring of massless particles has no wave, only the forced oscillation $h_{\rm forced}$ of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_sol}),
which, expressed as an inclination $|h_{\rm forced}|/r_V$, is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_inc}
    i_f = \frac{M_s}{M_p}\frac{\beta^2b_{3/2}^{(m)}(\beta)}{6(m - 1)|x|}\,i_s
\end{equation}
when the planet's oblateness is negligible. The simulation uses $J_2 = \vresJtwo$ and $R_p = \vresRp$, in
natural units where $G = M_p = 1$. Its ring is \vresNstreamlines\ streamlines of \vresNparticles\ particles each, spanning
$1 \le r \le \vresRmax$, and an $m = \vresM$ satellite with $i_s = \vresIs$ at $a_s = \vresAs$ puts $r_V = \vresRV$
mid-ring. The satellite's mass grows as $M_s = \vresMs(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = \vresTau$, or about
\vresTauOrbits\ orbits, slowly enough that the particles' motion is mostly forced rather than free, and what free
motion remains averages away over time. Figure \ref{fig:vres} shows that the particles' inclinations,
averaged over the last $\vresAvgOrbits$ of the simulation's $\vresOrbits$ orbits, are in excellent agreement with
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_inc}), to within \vresAgreePercent\%, and the differences are of order $x$, which
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_inc}) neglects. The test's
notebook\footnote{\url{https://github.com/joehahn/epi_int_3d/blob/main/tests/vertical_resonance/vertical_resonance.ipynb}}
also compares each particle's vertical motion over time to the exact solution, which the simulation
follows to within $\vresZerror$ in $z$.

The forced response also locates the resonance. A particle forced by Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_force}) oscillates as
$z = {\rm Re}[he^{im(\theta - \Omega_pt)}]$ where ${\rm Im}(h) = A_V/D$, so each streamline's amplitude $h$,
fit to its particles' $z$ and averaged over the last $\vresAvgOrbits$ orbits, gives the simulated
$D = A_V/{\rm Im}(h)$. Figure \ref{fig:resloc} shows that it agrees with the exact
$D$ at the streamline's semimajor axis to within \vresDpercent\%, so it vanishes where theory
puts the resonance, at $r_V$, and it departs from the linear approximation ${\cal D}x$ by terms of order $x^2$,
as theory does.

\subsubsection{A self-gravitating ring}
\label{sec:waves_ring}

%numbers from tests/bending_waves by tools/paper_numbers.py; figure by figures/bending_wave.py
The same kind of satellite also launches a spiral bending wave in a ring that has mass. This ring is
\bwNstreamlines\ streamlines of \bwNparticles\ particles each, spanning $1 \le r \le \bwRmax$, whose total
mass $\bwRingMass$ gives it the uniform surface density $\sigma = \bwSigma$. It has no viscosity or
pressure, and $J_2$ and $R_p$ are as in Section \ref{sec:waves_massless}. An $m = \bwM$ satellite of mass
$M_s = \bwMs$ and inclination $i_s = \bwIs$ at $a_s = \bwAs$ puts the resonance at $r_V = \bwRV$,
\bwAcrossPercent\% of the way across the ring, and the satellite is at full mass from $t = 0$. These
parameters do not correspond to any real ring and satellite. Rather, they were chosen so that the wave is
linear and well resolved: its amplitude $|Z_w|$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_amp}), keeps $|kZ_w| \le \bwkZ$
everywhere in the ring, and its wavelength, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_lambda}), spans about
\bwPerWavelength\ streamlines even at the ring's inner edge, where it is shortest.

The wave propagates inward at the speed $|c_g| = \pi G\sigma/\nu_a$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:bw_cg}) near resonance,
so it takes the time $\tau_c = (r_V - 1)/|c_g|$, about \bwCrossOrbits\ orbits, to cross from $r_V$ to the ring's inner edge. The
simulation stops at $\bwOrbits$ orbits, or $\bwRunFraction\,\tau_c$, before the wave can reflect there.
Figure \ref{fig:bwave} shows the wave being launched at $r_V$ and propagating inward.
Switching the
satellite on at full mass also starts a free vertical oscillation everywhere, so at first every streamline
moves as a massless particle would. The ring's self-gravity then takes over: the response near $r_V$
settles onto linear theory, and the wave front advances inward at about $|c_g|$. The front overshoots linear
theory a little, since it carries along the free oscillation that was started at resonance, and a small
residue of that oscillation remains ahead of the front. Behind the front, though, the simulated
inclinations are in excellent agreement with linear theory, with about \bwCrests\ wavelengths of the wave
in place by the end of the run. There the wave's amplitude is within \bwAmpPercent\% of $|Z_w|$, and its
phase winds inward as the trailing wave of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_sol}) does. On the nonwave side of the
resonance, the forced response is up to \bwForcedPercent\% larger than linear theory's. That theory is for
an infinite sheet, which has ring matter beyond this ring's outer edge, and that is the likely cause,
though it has not been quantified. The satellite pulls only vertically, and the wave leaves no mark in
the ring plane: no particle moves radially by more than $\bwRadial$, well under 1\% of the streamline
spacing $\Delta = \bwSpacing$.

Figure \ref{fig:wavenumber} shows the simulated wave's wavenumber $|k|$, measured from the radial gradient of
the phase of its wave part $h - L_V/x$, as in Fig.~5 of \cite{H07}. Behind the front it is \bwKcontPercent\%
larger than the $|k| = 2\pi/\lambda_{\rm wave}$ of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_lambda}) (the median), i.e., its wavelength is
that much shorter. This is because the simulated ring is a finite lattice of streamlines
rather than the infinite, continuous sheet that Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_lambda}) assumes, and both
differences weaken the wave's restoring force. Appendix \ref{sec:wire_continuum} shows that the lattice's
spacing reduces self-gravity's restoring force from $2\pi G\sigma|k|$ to $X(k\Delta)$, and that the ring's
edges reduce it further, by $2G\sigma(1/d_{\rm in} + 1/d_{\rm out})$. With the lattice allowed for, the
simulated wavenumber is \bwKlatPercent\% larger than expected, and with the edges allowed for too,
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_edges}), it is \bwKedgePercent\% larger. The test's
notebook\footnote{\url{https://github.com/joehahn/epi_int_3d/blob/main/tests/bending_waves/bending_waves.ipynb}}
also compares the wave's complex amplitude with linear theory.

The torque that the satellite exerts on the wave tests the wave's amplitude and phase together. The satellite's
resonant potential $A_Vz\sin m(\theta - \Omega_pt)$, whose vertical pull is
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_force}), exerts the torque $T = -mA_V\sum\mu z\cos m(\theta - \Omega_pt)$
on the simulated ring, summed over its particles of mass $\mu$, while linear theory gives \citep{S84}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:vres_torque}
    T_V = -\frac{\pi^2m\sigma r_V^2A_V^2}{\cal D},
\end{equation}
which is also the angular momentum luminosity of the wave that the satellite launches. Figure \ref{fig:torque}
shows that $T$ settles onto $T_V$ as soon as the wave is launched. Over the run's last half its mean is
$\bwTorqueRatio\,T_V$, and it scatters by at most \bwTorqueScatter\% about $T_V$ because of the residue of the free
oscillation. The satellite pulls only vertically, so the ring does not feel this torque, and its total
angular momentum $L_z$ can change only through the ring's gravity and the integrator's round-off error.
Figure \ref{fig:angmom} shows that $|\Delta L_z/L_z| \le \bwDeltaL$ over the run.

\section{Relation to \code{epi\_int} and \code{epi\_int\_lite}}
\label{sec:appendix_wire2d}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

%equations G3, G6, G10, G14 of notes/sympy/streamline_gravity.py; epi_int_lite issues 7 and 9
In the ring plane $d_z = s_z = 0$, and the acceleration of Section \ref{sec:wire} reduces to the geometry of Fig.~3 of \cite{HS13},
which is redrawn here as Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}. There the streamline's tilt
$\varphi$ from $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ has $\tan\varphi = s_r$. Then $c = d_r\sin\varphi\cos\varphi$
and $\boldsymbol{\Delta} = d_r\cos\varphi(\cos\varphi\hat{\mathbf{r}} - \sin\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$, i.e., the perpendicular has
length $\Delta = d_r\cos\varphi$ and makes the angle $\varphi$ with $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. The acceleration is
then
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:wire_2d}
\begin{eqnarray}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'}{d_r} \label{eqn:wire_2d_a}\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda'\tan\varphi}{d_r}. \label{eqn:wire_2d_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
Out of the plane, the tangential acceleration $A_{g,\theta} = -2G\lambda'c/\Delta^2$ of Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Acomp}) also includes the
term $-2G\lambda'd_zs_z/N^2\Delta^2$, which is due to the streamline's vertical tilt, and this is how
vertically displaced streamlines exchange angular momentum.

\cite{HS13} give the components of this acceleration as
$A_{g,r} = A_g\cos\varphi$ and $A_{g,\theta} = -A_g\sin\varphi$, with $A_g = 2G\lambda/\Delta$ and
$\Delta$ the perpendicular distance, and approximate them as $A_{g,r} \simeq A_g$ and
$A_{g,\theta} \simeq -A_gv_r'/v_\theta'$ (their Eqns.\ 20), to lowest order in the streamline's
eccentricity. With $\Delta$ read as the radial gap $d_r$ and $\tan\varphi = v_r'/v_\theta'$, those
are Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) without approximation. \code{Epi\_int\_lite} \citep{H25} differs from
Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) in three ways, which \code{epi\_int\_3d} corrects.

\paragraph{Distance} \code{Epi\_int\_lite} evaluates the pull's magnitude at the crossing point,
$\Delta = d_r$, but keeps its direction toward the near point,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eqn:wire_lite}
\begin{eqnarray}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda\cos\varphi}{d_r} \label{eqn:wire_lite_a}\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda\sin\varphi}{d_r}. \label{eqn:wire_lite_b}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
This pull is weaker than Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) by the factor $\cos\varphi = 1 -
{\cal O}(\varphi^2)$. Which is why \code{epi\_int} and \code{epi\_int\_lite} could use the distance
to the crossing point, $|\mathbf{d}_0|$, for $\Delta$, also out of the plane, i.e., neglect
$\mathbf{d}_1$ in the pull's magnitude. Section \ref{sec:wire} shows that the near point lies very
close to the crossing point, $|\mathbf{d}_1| \le s\Delta$ with $s \lesssim m(e^2 + i^2)^{1/2}$, and
inserting that into $|\mathbf{d}_0|^2 = \Delta^2 + |\mathbf{d}_1|^2$ (Eqn.\ \ref{eqn:wire_pythag})
gives $|\mathbf{d}_0|/N \le \Delta \le |\mathbf{d}_0|$. So the two differ by a fraction of order
$s^2 \lesssim m^2(e^2 + i^2)$, which is $\lesssim\!10^{-4}$ even for $m(e^2 + i^2)^{1/2}$ as large as
$10^{-2}$ and so of no practical consequence, but Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) cost no more.

\paragraph{Direction} \code{Epi\_int\_lite} takes the wire's direction from the streamline's velocity at
the crossing point, $\sin\varphi = v_r'/v'$ and $\cos\varphi = v_\theta'/v'$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_velocity}). The velocity is tangent to the streamline only when the
streamline's shape is stationary. If the shape is a pattern that rotates at $\Omega_p$ while its
particles orbit at $\Omega_a$, then $v_r'/v_\theta' = (1 - \Omega_p/\Omega_a)s_r$ where $s_r$ is the
slope of the streamline's shape (Eqn.\ \ref{eqn:wire_slopes}). Near an $m$-armed Lindblad resonance
$1 - \Omega_p/\Omega_a \simeq \pm1/m$, so the velocity underestimates the wire's tilt, and with it the
tangential pull that transports angular momentum between streamlines, by a factor of about $m$. For
the slowly precessing $m = 1$ ringlets of \cite{H25}, $\Omega_p$ is the precession rate and the error
is negligible. \code{Epi\_int\_3d} takes the direction from the shape (Eqn.\ \ref{eqn:wire_t}).

\paragraph{Linear density} \code{Epi\_int\_lite} evaluates every perturbing streamline's pull with the
perturbed particle's own linear density $\lambda$, rather than the perturbing streamline's
$\lambda'$. The difference, $\lambda' - \lambda \simeq (a' - a)\partial\lambda/\partial a$, adds a
nearly uniform pull of order the ring's mass over the planet's; in the eccentric ringlets of
\cite{H25} it changes the precession rate by about $10^{-7}$ of itself.

In an eccentric ringlet the three corrections together change the streamlines' eccentricities by less
than $10^{-4}$ of themselves over 5000 orbits. Only the second matters for spiral waves, where it
changes the torque between streamlines by a factor of about $m$. As in \code{epi\_int\_lite}, the
particle's own streamline exerts no force on it in \code{epi\_int\_3d}, and $\Delta$ never vanishes, since
streamlines may not cross. The slopes require that the streamline be well sampled by its particles.
\code{Epi\_int\_3d} also keeps
\code{epi\_int\_lite}'s ring gravity, Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_lite}), as an option, so that its results can be
checked against \code{epi\_int\_lite}'s exactly.

\section{Resolution limit of the streamline method}
\label{sec:appendix_zigzag}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

%draft (2026-10-18); equations are sympy-checked P6, P7 in notes/sympy/ring_pressure.py
A streamline model represents a continuous ring by $N_r$ streamlines spaced $\Delta$ apart, and
that discreteness sets which wavelengths of the ring's response it can follow. This appendix
derives the axisymmetric stability of a uniform lattice of circular streamlines under the
self-gravity and pressure accelerations of \code{epi\_int\_lite} \citep{HS13, H25}, and compares it
with the continuum Toomre criterion.

Each streamline of linear density $\lambda$ exerts the acceleration $2G\lambda/d$ of a straight
wire on a particle a distance $d$ away \citep{HS13}. The pressure $p = c^2\sigma$ acts through
the surface density $\sigma_j = 2\lambda/(r_{j+1} - r_{j-1})$ of streamline $j$ and the central
difference $A_j = -(p_{j+1} - p_{j-1})/2\lambda$, which tends to $-\sigma^{-1}\partial p/\partial r$
as $\Delta \rightarrow 0$. Displace the streamlines radially by $\xi_j = \xi\cos(j\phi)$ where
$\phi = k\Delta$ and $k$ is the radial wavenumber, so that $0 < \phi \le \pi$; $\phi = \pi$ is
the shortest wave the lattice can carry, the zigzag $\xi_j = (-1)^j\xi$ of
Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}a. Linearizing in $\xi$, the streamlines at $j = \pm n$ add
$4G\lambda(1 - \cos n\phi)\xi/(n\Delta)^2$ to the acceleration of streamline 0, and summing over
$n$ with $\sigma = \lambda/\Delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_X}
    X(\phi) = \frac{4G\sigma}{\Delta}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1 - \cos n\phi}{n^2}
            = \frac{4G\sigma}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\pi\phi}{2} - \frac{\phi^2}{4}\right),
\end{equation}
while pressure contributes
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_p}
    \omega_p^2(\phi) = \left(\frac{c}{\Delta}\right)^2\sin^2\phi,
\end{equation}
so that each mode oscillates at the frequency
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_disp}
    \omega^2 = \kappa^2 - X(\phi) + \omega_p^2(\phi).
\end{equation}
For $\phi \ll 1$ these recover the continuum terms $X \rightarrow 2\pi G\sigma k$ and
$\omega_p^2 \rightarrow c^2k^2$, and Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_disp}) becomes the dispersion relation
of a pressure-supported self-gravitating sheet, which is stable at all $k$ when
$Q = c\kappa/\pi G\sigma \ge 1$ \citep{T64, BT87}. At the grid scale the lattice departs from
the continuum in two ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}b). Self-gravity peaks at
$X(\pi) = \pi^2G\sigma/\Delta$, half the continuum value $2\pi G\sigma k$ at $k = \pi/\Delta$.
And pressure vanishes there: in the zigzag, streamlines $j \pm 1$ move together, so
$r_{j+1} - r_{j-1}$ and hence $\sigma_j$ and $p_j$ do not change. The viscous fluxes of
\cite{HS13}, which are differenced in the same way, likewise exert no force on the zigzag.

A cold continuum disk ($c = 0$) is unstable to all wavelengths shorter than
$\lambda_{\rm crit} = 4\pi^2G\sigma/\kappa^2$, and the lattice hides these: by
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_X}) a cold lattice is axisymmetrically stable as long as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_R}
    {\cal R} \equiv \frac{\lambda_{\rm crit}}{2\Delta} = \frac{2\pi^2G\sigma}{\kappa^2\Delta}
    \simeq \frac{\pi G m_r N_r}{\kappa^2 a\Delta a^2} < 2,
\end{equation}
where $2\Delta$ is the shortest resolved wavelength, and the last form is for a ringlet of mass
$m_r$, semimajor axis $a$ and width $\Delta a$, with $\sigma \simeq m_r/2\pi a\Delta a$ and
$\Delta \simeq \Delta a/N_r$. Adding streamlines to a given ringlet raises ${\cal R}$ in
proportion to $N_r$.

%O1-O5, sympy-checked in notes/sympy/viscous_overstability.py
A viscous ringlet must also avoid the viscous overstability, the growth of axisymmetric epicyclic
oscillations that viscous stresses drive when the shear stress rises steeply enough with surface
density \citep{ST95, LR95, SSS01, S01, LO06, LO08}. For a cold ringlet with constant kinematic shear
and bulk viscosities $\nu_s$ and $\nu_b$, as in \code{epi\_int\_lite}, and Keplerian $\kappa = \Omega$, a
mode with oscillation frequency $\omega \simeq \kappa(1 - x)^{1/2}$ grows to first order in the
viscosities at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:overstability}
    \gamma = \frac{K}{6(1 - x)}\left[\nu_s(4x + 2) - 3\nu_b(1 - x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $K = k^2$ and $x = 2\pi G\sigma k/\kappa^2$ in the continuum. Without self-gravity ($x = 0$)
the ringlet is stable when $\nu_b > 2\nu_s/3$, the onset found by \cite{ST95} and \cite{LSS17} for a
viscosity independent of $\sigma$, while self-gravity is destabilizing \citep{LSS17}: a mode is
stable only for $x < (3\nu_b - 2\nu_s)/(4\nu_s + 3\nu_b)$, which is $1/7$ for $\nu_b = \nu_s$ and
$0.4$ for $\nu_b = 2\nu_s$. The streamline lattice follows Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:overstability}) with the
grid's versions of both terms: $K = \sin^2\phi/\Delta^2$, from the code's central differences of the
viscous stresses, and $x = X(\phi)/\kappa^2$. Both stresses vanish at the zigzag, $\phi = \pi$, where
self-gravity is strongest, $X(\pi)/\kappa^2 = {\cal R}/2$; so a viscous lattice is stable at every
wavelength when ${\cal R} < 2/7$ for $\nu_b = \nu_s$, or ${\cal R} < 4/5$ for $\nu_b = 2\nu_s$, more
stringent than Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}). Since $K\nu \sim \nu/\Delta^2$, an overstable lattice's
modes grow faster the more streamlines it has.

That limit matters because a ringlet's self-gravity must be strong enough to resist the
differential precession due to $J_2$. Across a ringlet of width $\Delta a$, $J_2$ drives a
differential precession $\propto J_2(R_p/a)^2\Omega_a\Delta a/a$, while for a small nonlinearity
parameter the ringlet's self-gravity drives one $\propto (m_r/M_p)\Omega_a\Delta\tilde{\omega}(a/\Delta a)^2$,
where $\Delta\tilde{\omega}$ is the periapse twist across it (cf.\ Eqn.\ C1 of \citealt{H25}).
%TODO: this scaling is an order-of-magnitude estimate that fits the runs below; derive it properly
So keeping $J_2$ from winding up the ringlet requires $m_r \propto J_2\Delta a^3/\Delta\tilde{\omega}$,
and then ${\cal R} \propto J_2\Delta a N_r/\Delta\tilde{\omega}$: a wider ringlet does not help, and
for a given planet more streamlines can be afforded only by a narrower ringlet. This is tested
here with simulations of a ringlet of width $\Delta a = 0.0125a$ whose edges are tapered, mass $m_r = 3\times10^{-8}M_p$,
eccentricity $e = 10^{-4}$, periapses twisted by $\pi$ from its inner to outer edge, and kinematic
viscosity $\nu = 7\times10^{-11}a^2\Omega_a$, with no pressure, $R_p = 0.5a$ and $J_2 = 0.01$. With
$N_r = 100$ streamlines (${\cal R} \simeq 0.09$ in the ringlet's middle, where the taper makes
$\sigma$ 1.25 times and $\Delta$ 0.8 times their ringlet-wide means) the ringlet stays smooth for $10^4$ orbits while
self-gravity, rather than $J_2$, controls its shape: within about 1000 orbits it
straightens the initial twist, and the ringlet's body then stays nearly aligned, as two sub-ringlets that precess slowly relative to each other. With $N_r = 300$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.3$) and
$N_r = 1000$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.9$) it becomes ragged and its streamlines cross after about
190 and 30 orbits. At $N_r = 1000$ the disturbance starts in the ringlet's interior, grows at the
same rate with 40 or 241 particles per streamline, and does not grow when ring gravity is turned
off, consistent with the self-gravity-assisted overstability for $\nu_b = \nu_s$ at
${\cal R} > 2/7$. Pressure with $Q \le 2$ delays the crossing by less than two orbits, as expected
from Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}b, where $\omega_p^2 \le 0.1\kappa^2$.

The crossings near ${\cal R} \simeq 0.3$ are mostly a different, numerical, problem. The same
ringlet without the initial twist, at $N_r = 333$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.31$), crosses after 145
orbits, and its code's own linearized axisymmetric modes, computed from the reference implementation's
accelerations, grow by only $3.3\times10^{-3}$ per orbit, far too slowly. The disturbance that crosses is instead a sawtooth
along each streamline with azimuthal wavenumber $m \simeq N_t/2$ where $N_t$ is the number of
particles per streamline. It also grows in a circular ringlet, without ring gravity, and without
viscosity, though more slowly (after about 220, 170 and 850 orbits), its onset varies
non-monotonically with $N_t$ (with $N_t = 30$ or 50 the ringlet survives $10^4$ orbits, with 20, 40
or 320 it does not), and pressure with $Q \le 10$ delays it by at most 20\%. It is presumably seeded by
the 3-point Lagrange interpolation in longitude that couples neighboring streamlines' particles,
and it remains an open problem.

So Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}) is a necessary condition only. In practice a simulated ringlet whose
self-gravity matters should be run with ${\cal R} \lesssim 0.1$, viscous or not, and a ringlet that
needs more streamlines must be made narrower and much less massive.

\section{The restoring force of a bending wave}
\label{sec:wire_continuum}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

Equation (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) recovers the self-gravity of a bending wave. Consider a sheet of
surface density $\sigma$ made of streamlines with $\lambda = \sigma\,dr'$, displaced vertically to
$Z(r) = Z_0\cos kr$. When $|d_z| \ll |d_r|$ and the slopes are small,
$A_{g,z} \simeq 2G\lambda d_z/d_r^2$, and the sum of $A_{g,z}$ over the sheet is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_sheet}
    A_z = 2G\sigma Z_0\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos kx - 1}{x^2}\,dx = -2\pi G\sigma|k|Z,
\end{equation}
which is the self-gravity term of the bending-wave dispersion relation,
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:bw_disp}) \citep{SCL83, S84}. So summing the wires'
pulls over the streamlines gives the vertical restoring force of a self-gravitating sheet, just
as the same sum gives the $-2\pi G\sigma|k|$ term of a density wave in the plane. For bending waves,
though, self-gravity is restoring, and adds to $\nu_a^2$.

On the code's lattice of streamlines spaced $\Delta$ apart (here $\Delta$ is the spacing, as in
Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_zigzag}), with $Z_j = Z_0\cos(j\phi)$ and
$\phi = k\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_lattice}
    A_z = \frac{4G\sigma Z_0}{\Delta}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\cos n\phi - 1}{n^2} = -X(\phi)Z_0,
\end{equation}
where $X(\phi)$ is the lattice self-gravity of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_X}). The vertical lattice
modes therefore oscillate at $\omega^2 = \nu_a^2 + X(\phi)$, which tends to the continuum value as
$\phi \rightarrow 0$. At the zigzag $\phi = \pi$, the extra restoring force is half the continuum
value, as it is in the plane. Vertically, however, the lattice's self-gravity is stabilizing, so
the resolution limit of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}), which concerns in-plane modes, has no vertical
counterpart.

A ring of finite width also lacks the streamlines beyond its edges. Consider a streamline whose $N$th
neighbor on one side would be the first one beyond the ring's edge. The sum of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_lattice})
then stops at $n = N - 1$ on that side, and the missing terms are
$(2G\sigma Z_0/\Delta)\sum_{n \ge N}(\cos n\phi - 1)/n^2$. Their cosines nearly cancel when the wave is
shorter than the distance to the edge, while $\sum_{n \ge N}1/n^2 \simeq 1/(N - \frac{1}{2})$, so the
missing pull is about $2G\sigma Z_0/d$ where $d = (N - \frac{1}{2})\Delta$ is the streamline's distance
to the ring's edge plus half a spacing. Near $r_V$, where $-D \simeq {\cal D}|x|$ on the
wave side, the lattice's restoring force so replaces the continuum's ${\cal D}|x| = 2\pi G\sigma|k|$ with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_edges}
    {\cal D}|x| = X(k\Delta) - 2G\sigma\left(\frac{1}{d_{\rm in}} + \frac{1}{d_{\rm out}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $d_{\rm in}$ and $d_{\rm out}$ are the distances to the ring's inner and outer edges, so
measured. Both terms lower the restoring force at a given $k$, so the wave's wavenumber is larger than in
the continuum, by the amounts that Section \ref{sec:waves_ring} finds.

\section{The massless limit of the bending wave}
\label{sec:massless_limit}
\setcounter{equation}{0}

%equations B16, sympy-checked in notes/sympy/bending_wave.py
Equation (\ref{eqn:vres_h}) tends to the forced oscillation $h_{\rm forced} = L_V/x$ as the ring's surface
density $\sigma \rightarrow 0$, which sends $\epsilon \rightarrow 0$. On the outer side of the resonance,
$x > 0$, integrate the integral in Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_h}) by parts using
$e^{is^2/2\epsilon} = (\epsilon/is)\,d(e^{is^2/2\epsilon})/ds$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:limit_parts}
    \int_x^\infty e^{is^2/2\epsilon}\,ds = -\frac{\epsilon}{ix}e^{ix^2/2\epsilon}
        + \frac{\epsilon}{i}\int_x^\infty \frac{e^{is^2/2\epsilon}}{s^2}\,ds.
\end{equation}
Integrating the last term by parts the same way shows that it is of order $\epsilon^2/x^3$, smaller than the
first by a factor of order $\epsilon/x^2$. Inserting the first term into Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_h}) then gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:limit_outer}
    h = \frac{L_V}{x}\left[1 + {\cal O}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{x^2}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
so $h \rightarrow h_{\rm forced}$ as $\sigma \rightarrow 0$ at any fixed $x > 0$.

On the inner side, $x < 0$, the range of integration passes through $s = 0$, where $\epsilon/is$ diverges, so
write the integral as $\int_{-\infty}^\infty - \int_{-\infty}^x$. The first is the Fresnel integral
$(2\pi\epsilon)^{1/2}e^{i\pi/4}$, and the second integrates by parts as above, so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:limit_inner}
    h = \frac{L_V}{x}\left[1 + {\cal O}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{x^2}\right)\right]
        - ie^{i\pi/4}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\epsilon}\right)^{1/2}L_Ve^{-ix^2/2\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
The second term is $h_{\rm wave}$ of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_sol_wave}), with the amplitude $|Z_w|$ of
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_amp}), and it grows as $\sigma^{-1/2}$ as $\sigma \rightarrow 0$. This is because
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_h}) is a steady state: the wave has had unlimited time to fill the ring inside $r_V$,
and the satellite's torque on it, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:vres_torque}), which is proportional to $\sigma$, is carried
away at the speed $|c_g| = \pi G\sigma/\nu_a$, which also is proportional to $\sigma$. So the wave's
angular momentum per unit area, which is proportional to $\sigma|Z_w|^2$, does not depend on $\sigma$. At any finite time $t$, though, the wave
has reached only $x \simeq -|c_g|t/r_V$, which tends to zero with $\sigma$, so the inner side too has only
the forced oscillation $h_{\rm forced}$ as $\sigma \rightarrow 0$. The massless ring of
Section \ref{sec:waves_massless} is this limit.
