\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

%placeholder
The epi\_int and epi\_int\_lite N-body integrators \citep{HS13, H25} model a planetary ring as
a set of gravitating streamlines whose particles are confined to the ring plane. Spiral bending
waves, which are launched at a satellite's vertical resonances \citep{SCL83, S84}, require the
particles' vertical motions too. This paper describes epi\_int\_3d, a modest extension of
epi\_int\_lite into the vertical (out-of-plane) direction, and uses it to simulate spiral
bending waves.


\section{Vertical epicyclic motion}
\label{sec:vertical}

The epi\_int\_lite drift step advances each particle's unperturbed orbit about an oblate planet
using first-order epicyclic equations that are exactly reversible: converting spatial
coordinates into epicyclic orbit elements and back introduces no error, so the drift step
is not a source of numerical drift \citep{H25}. Here those equations are extended to
particles having vertical displacement $z$
and vertical velocity $v_z$, with the same first-order accuracy and the same exact
reversibility.

\subsection{Equations of motion}
\label{sec:eom}

A particle orbiting an oblate planet obeys Newton's second law,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:newton}
    \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\nabla\Phi,
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ is the planet's gravitational potential. That potential is a series in the
planet's zonal harmonics $J_{2k}$ (Eqn.\ 5 of \citealt{BL94}), and as in \cite{HS13} and
\cite{H25} only the $J_2$ term is kept, so
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:Phi3d}
    \Phi(r,z) = -\frac{GM_p}{\rho}\left[1 - J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{\rho}\right)^2P_2\left(\frac{z}{\rho}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Here $M_p$ is the planet's mass, $R_p$ its radius, $r$ the particle's cylindrical radius,
$\rho^2 = r^2 + z^2$, and $P_2(\mu) = (3\mu^2 - 1)/2$. In cylindrical coordinates
$(r, \theta, z)$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:newton}) is
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:eom_cyl}
    \ddot{r} - r\dot{\theta}^2 &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r} \nonumber\\
    \frac{d}{dt}\left(r^2\dot{\theta}\right) &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\theta} = 0 \nonumber\\
    \ddot{z} &=& -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}.
\end{eqnarray}
The potential is independent of $\theta$, so the $z$-component of the specific angular
momentum, $h = r^2\dot{\theta} = rv_\theta$, is conserved exactly, even when $z \ne 0$.
The potential is also even in $z$, so near the ring plane
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:Phi_expand}
    \Phi(r,z) = \Phi(r,0) + \frac{1}{2}\nu^2(r)z^2 + {\cal O}(z^4),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(r,0) = -GM_p/r - J_2GM_pR_p^2/2r^3$ is the midplane potential and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:nu_r}
    \nu^2(r) = \left.\frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2}\right|_{z=0}
        = \frac{GM_p}{r^3}\left[1 + \frac{9}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{r}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}
Equations (\ref{eqn:eom_cyl}) then become
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:eom_expand}
    \ddot{r} &=& \frac{h^2}{r^3} - \frac{\partial\Phi(r,0)}{\partial r} + {\cal O}(z^2) \nonumber\\
    \ddot{z} &=& -\nu^2(r)z + {\cal O}(z^3).
\end{eqnarray}

To first order the radial equation does not involve $z$; it is the planar equation of
motion. \cite{H25} solved the radial part of Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:eom_expand}) to first order in
small displacements from circular motion, in a form that is exactly reversible: the
conversion from the coordinates $r, \theta, v_r, v_\theta$ to the epicyclic elements
$a, e, \tilde{\omega}, M$, and back, is exact. This is required if the solution is to be
used in the drift step of a drift-kick N-body integrator. That solution is summarized in
Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_inplane}. In it the particle's radius is $r = a[1 + {\cal O}(e)]$,
its mean longitude $\lambda = \tilde{\omega} + (\Omega_a/\kappa_a)M$ advances at the mean
angular velocity $\Omega_a$, and its mean anomaly $M$ advances at the epicyclic frequency
$\kappa_a$. Note that $\Omega$ denotes the longitude of ascending node, while $\Omega_a$ is
the mean angular velocity.

So the only additional physics that epi\_int\_lite's drift step needs in order to follow
out-of-plane motion is the vertical equation of motion, the second of
Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:eom_expand}); the vertical forces that the ring and satellites exert enter
the kick step and are treated separately. Evaluating $\nu^2(r)$ at $r = a$ reduces the
vertical equation to the harmonic oscillator
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:z_oscillator}
    \ddot{z} = -\nu_a^2 z,
\end{equation}
with vertical frequency (Eqn.\ 17 of \citealt{BL94})
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:nu}
    \nu_a \equiv \nu(a) = \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 + \frac{9}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where the neglected terms are of order ${\cal O}(ei)$, due to the particle's radial excursion
$r - a = {\cal O}(ea)$, and ${\cal O}(i^3)$. Outside the planet the potential obeys Laplace's equation, which for an axisymmetric
potential is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:laplace}
    \nabla^2\Phi = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}\right)
        + \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial z^2} = 0.
\end{equation}
At $r = a$ and $z = 0$, where $\Omega_a^2 = r^{-1}\partial\Phi/\partial r$,
$\kappa_a^2 = 3\Omega_a^2 + \partial^2\Phi/\partial r^2$ and
$\nu_a^2 = \partial^2\Phi/\partial z^2$, Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:laplace}) relates the three
frequencies via $\kappa_a^2 + \nu_a^2 = 2\Omega_a^2$.

\subsection{Vertical solution}
\label{sec:elem2coords}

The solution of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:z_oscillator}) is a sinusoid of frequency $\nu_a$ whose
amplitude and phase define two new orbit elements, the inclination $i$ and the longitude of
ascending node $\Omega$:
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:zvz}
    z   &=& a\,i\sin(\lambda - \Omega) \nonumber\\
    v_z &=& a\nu_a i\cos(\lambda - \Omega),
\end{eqnarray}
where the node regresses at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:node}
    \dot{\Omega} = \Omega_a - \nu_a \simeq -\frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\Omega_a.
\end{equation}
The vertical phase $\lambda - \Omega$ then advances at exactly $\nu_a$, so
Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) solve Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:z_oscillator}) exactly, with $v_z = \dot{z}$,
and the particle crosses the ring plane upward at longitude $\lambda = \Omega$.
Measuring the phase from $\lambda$ rather than $\theta$ is what keeps the vertical motion
a pure harmonic oscillator; $\theta - \Omega$ would add an ${\cal O}(ei)$ error to the phase.

\subsection{Coordinates to elements}
\label{sec:coords2elem}

Equations (\ref{eqn:zvz}) are inverted by a fifth step that follows steps 1--4 of
Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_inplane}. Step 1 remains exact in three dimensions because $h$
is conserved and $r$ is the cylindrical radius, so steps 1--4 provide $a$, $\Omega_a$,
$\kappa_a$, $e$, $M$, $\tilde{\omega}$, and so $\lambda$. Step 5 computes $\nu_a$ from
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:nu}) and then
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:i_node}
    i &=& \sqrt{\left(\frac{z}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{v_z}{a\nu_a}\right)^2} \nonumber\\
    \Omega &=& \lambda - \text{atan2}(z, v_z/\nu_a).
\end{eqnarray}
Once $a$ is known, the mapping $(i, \lambda - \Omega) \leftrightarrow (z, v_z/\nu_a)$ is a
change between polar and Cartesian coordinates, so Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) and
(\ref{eqn:i_node}) are exact inverses. The drift step from $t$ to $t + \Delta t$ converts
coordinates to elements, advances $M \rightarrow M + \kappa_a\Delta t$ and
$\Omega \rightarrow \Omega + (\Omega_a - \nu_a)\Delta t$, and converts back. Its vertical part
is simply a rotation of $(z, v_z/\nu_a)$ through the angle $\nu_a\Delta t$.


\section{The gravity of a streamline}
\label{sec:gravity}

%equations G1-G17, sympy-checked in notes/sympy/streamline_gravity.py; figures by figures/wire_planar.py
Epi\_int\_3d assigns its particles to streamlines, each a closed eccentric path around the planet
whose particles share a common semimajor axis $a$, and a streamline is labeled by that semimajor
axis, like its predecessors epi\_int and epi\_int\_lite. All epi\_int\_3d
simulations span only a narrow annulus within a broader planetary ring, so every particle is
radially close to the nearby portions of all perturbing streamlines. Which is why all three codes
assume that the gravitational acceleration of a particle by the particles inhabiting the perturbing
streamline at semimajor axis $a'$ is that due to a straight and infinitely long wire:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_Ag}
    \mathbf{A}_g = \frac{2G\lambda'}{\Delta}\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda'$ is the perturbing streamline's linear density, $\Delta$ is the particle's distance
from the streamline's nearest point, which is labeled the near point in Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar},
and $\hat{\boldsymbol{\Delta}} = \boldsymbol{\Delta}/\Delta$ is the unit vector that points from the
particle toward the near point.

\subsection{The perturbing streamline's geometry}
\label{sec:wire}

The following 3D treatment is an extension of the 2D force calculations used in \cite{HS13}.
In cylindrical coordinates the particle is at $\mathbf{r} = (r, \theta, z)$. But in the
following the perturbing streamline's coordinates are to be regarded as functions of its longitude
$\theta'$, i.e.,
$\mathbf{r}' = (r'(\theta'), \theta', z'(\theta'))$.
Streamline $a'$ is sampled by its particles, so Lagrange polynomials are fit to the
three particles in $a'$ that are nearest in longitude, which allows $r'(\theta')$,
$z'(\theta')$ and the linear density $\lambda'(\theta')$ to be regarded as smooth
functions of $\theta'$. And because the particle is close to the streamline, $|r' - r| \ll r$,
$|z' - z| \ll r$ and $|\theta' - \theta| \ll 1$, so the particle's local frame
$(\hat{r}, \hat{\theta}, \hat{z})$ can be treated as Cartesian.

The vector from the particle to the streamline's near point (the black square in
Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}) is then
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_d}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} = \mathbf{r}'(\theta + \delta\theta') - \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{d}_0 + \mathbf{d}_1,
\end{equation}
where $\delta\theta'$ is the near point's longitude relative to the particle's longitude, as measured
in the $z = 0$ plane. This splits
the trip to the near point across two legs. The first leg, $\mathbf{d}_0 = \mathbf{r}'(\theta) - \mathbf{r}$,
travels from the particle to the crossing point $\mathbf{r}'(\theta) = (r'(\theta), \theta, z'(\theta))$, which is where the streamline crosses the
particle's longitude (the cross in Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}), so $\mathbf{d}_0 = (d_r, 0, d_z)$ in the local frame, where
$d_r = r'(\theta) - r$ and $d_z = z'(\theta) - z$. The second leg,
$\mathbf{d}_1 = \mathbf{r}'(\theta + \delta\theta') - \mathbf{r}'(\theta)$, then runs along the streamline from
the crossing point to the near point. That stretch of streamline is short and locally straight, so
$\mathbf{d}_1 \simeq [r'(\theta + \delta\theta') - r'(\theta), r\,\delta\theta', z'(\theta + \delta\theta') - z'(\theta)] \simeq
(s_r, 1, s_z)\,r\,\delta\theta' = N r\,\delta\theta'\,\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ to first order in
$\delta\theta'$, where the streamline's unit tangent is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_t}
    \hat{\mathbf{t}} = \frac{(s_r, 1, s_z)}{N}, \qquad N^2 = 1 + s_r^2 + s_z^2,
\end{equation}
and its slopes are
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_slopes}
    s_r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{dr'}{d\theta'}, \qquad
    s_z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{dz'}{d\theta'}
\end{equation}
at $\theta' = \theta$, which are taken from the derivatives of the Lagrange polynomials.

The near point is where $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ is perpendicular to the streamline,
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} = 0$. With $\mathbf{d}_1 = N r\,\delta\theta'\,
\hat{\mathbf{t}}$, that condition becomes $\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}} + N r\,\delta\theta' = 0$,
so the near point lies at $\delta\theta' = -c/r$, where $c = (\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})/N =
(d_rs_r + d_zs_z)/N^2$, and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_D}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} = \mathbf{d}_0 - (\mathbf{d}_0\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}})\hat{\mathbf{t}}
    = \hat{\mathbf{t}}\times(\mathbf{d}_0\times\hat{\mathbf{t}}),
\end{equation}
which is simply $\mathbf{d}_0$ with its component along the streamline removed. In components,
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:wire_Dcomp}
    \boldsymbol{\Delta} &=& (d_r - cs_r, -c, d_z - cs_z) \nonumber\\
    \Delta^2 &=& d_r^2 + d_z^2 - c(d_rs_r + d_zs_z).
\end{eqnarray}
Note that $|c| \le |d_rs_r| + |d_zs_z| \ll r$, so $|\delta\theta'| \ll 1$ as assumed, and the near
point lies much closer to the crossing point than the spacing of the streamline's particles.
Inserting $\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ into Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) then provides the acceleration that
streamline $a'$ exerts on the particle.

In the ring plane $d_z = s_z = 0$, and the above reduces to the geometry of Fig.~3 of \cite{HS13},
which is redrawn here as Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}. Let $\varphi$ be the angle at which the
streamline crosses $\hat{\theta}$, so that $s_r = \tan\varphi$. Then $c = d_r\sin\varphi\cos\varphi$
and $\boldsymbol{\Delta} = d_r\cos\varphi(\cos\varphi, -\sin\varphi, 0)$, i.e., the perpendicular has
length $\Delta = d_r\cos\varphi$ and makes the angle $\varphi$ with $\hat{r}$. The acceleration is
then
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:wire_2d}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'}{d_r} \nonumber\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda'\tan\varphi}{d_r}.
\end{eqnarray}
Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_wire2d} relates these to the in-plane accelerations computed by epi\_int
and epi\_int\_lite. Out of the plane, the tangential acceleration $A_{g,\theta} = -2G\lambda'c/\Delta^2$ also includes the
term $-2G\lambda'd_zs_z/N^2\Delta^2$, which is due to the streamline's vertical tilt, and this is how
vertically displaced streamlines exchange angular momentum.

In practice, epi\_int\_3d computes the acceleration $\mathbf{A}_g$ as follows. The particles in every
streamline are kept sorted by longitude, so the three particles of streamline $a'$ whose indices are
those of the perturbed particle and its two neighbors bracket the particle's longitude $\theta$.
Lagrange polynomials fit to those three particles then provide, by interpolation to $\theta$, the
crossing point's $r'(\theta)$ and $z'(\theta)$ and hence $d_r$ and $d_z$, the linear density
$\lambda'(\theta)$, and, from the polynomials' derivatives, the slopes $s_r$ and $s_z$ of
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_slopes}). Then
$N^2 = 1 + s_r^2 + s_z^2$ and $c = (d_rs_r + d_zs_z)/N^2$, Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Dcomp}) provide
$\boldsymbol{\Delta}$ and $\Delta^2$, and Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) provides the acceleration's three
components,
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:wire_Acomp}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'(d_r - cs_r)}{\Delta^2} \nonumber\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda'c}{\Delta^2} \nonumber\\
    A_{g,z} &=& \frac{2G\lambda'(d_z - cs_z)}{\Delta^2}.
\end{eqnarray}
This is repeated for every perturbing streamline, and the sum is the particle's acceleration due to
ring gravity. Note that $\lambda'$ is evaluated at the crossing point rather than at the near point,
which differs from it by a fraction of order $|\delta\theta'|$.

\subsection{The restoring force of a bending wave}
\label{sec:wire_continuum}

Equation (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}) recovers the self-gravity of a bending wave. Consider a sheet of
surface density $\sigma$ made of streamlines with $\lambda = \sigma\,dr'$, displaced vertically to
$Z(r) = Z_0\cos kr$. When $|d_z| \ll |d_r|$ and the slopes are small,
$A_{g,z} \simeq 2G\lambda d_z/d_r^2$, and the sum of $A_{g,z}$ over the sheet is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_sheet}
    A_z = 2G\sigma Z_0\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos kx - 1}{x^2}\,dx = -2\pi G\sigma|k|Z,
\end{equation}
which is the self-gravity term of the bending-wave dispersion relation,
$(\omega - m\Omega_a)^2 = \nu_a^2 + 2\pi G\sigma|k|$ \citep{SCL83, S84}. So summing the wires'
pulls over the streamlines gives the vertical restoring force of a self-gravitating sheet, just
as the same sum gives the $-2\pi G\sigma|k|$ term of a density wave in the plane. For bending waves,
though, self-gravity is restoring, and adds to $\nu_a^2$.

On the code's lattice of streamlines spaced $\Delta$ apart (here $\Delta$ is the spacing, as in
Appendix \ref{sec:appendix_zigzag}), with $Z_j = Z_0\cos(j\phi)$ and
$\phi = k\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:wire_lattice}
    A_z = \frac{4G\sigma Z_0}{\Delta}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\cos n\phi - 1}{n^2} = -X(\phi)Z_0,
\end{equation}
where $X(\phi)$ is the lattice self-gravity of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_X}). The vertical lattice
modes therefore oscillate at $\omega^2 = \nu_a^2 + X(\phi)$, which tends to the continuum value as
$\phi \rightarrow 0$. At the zigzag $\phi = \pi$, the extra restoring force is half the continuum
value, as it is in the plane. Vertically, however, the lattice's self-gravity is stabilizing, so
the resolution limit of Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}), which concerns in-plane modes, has no vertical
counterpart.

\label{sec:wire_code}

Epi\_int\_3d computes Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_Ag}), with $\lambda'$ the perturbing streamline's own
linear density at the particle's longitude, and both slopes $s_r$ and $s_z$ taken from the derivatives
of the Lagrange polynomials that give $r'$ and $z'$ there. In the plane it is

particle's own streamline exerts no force on it. The slopes require that the streamline be well
sampled by its particles.

\section{The in-plane solution of Hahn et al.\ (2025)}
\label{sec:appendix_inplane}

This appendix summarizes the first-order, exactly reversible solution of the planar equation
of motion from Appendix A of \cite{H25}, in the notation used here. The mean angular velocity
and epicyclic frequency at semimajor axis $a$ are
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:Omega_kappa}
    \Omega_a &=& \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 + \frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2} \nonumber\\
    \kappa_a &=& \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 - \frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R_p}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2}.
\end{eqnarray}
In terms of the epicyclic elements $a$, $e$, $\tilde{\omega}$ and $M$, the particle's
coordinates are
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:inplane}
    r &=& \frac{a}{\sqrt{1 + 2e\cos M}} \nonumber\\
    \theta &=& \frac{\Omega_a}{\kappa_a}(M + 2e\sin M) + \tilde{\omega} \nonumber\\
    v_r &=& ea\kappa_a\sin M\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^3 \nonumber\\
    v_\theta &=& \frac{a^2\Omega_a}{r},
\end{eqnarray}
which conserve $h = rv_\theta = a^2\Omega_a$ exactly and solve the planar equation of motion
with errors of order ${\cal O}(e^2)$. The elements advance as $M \rightarrow M + \kappa_a\Delta t$,
with $a$, $e$ and $\tilde{\omega}$ constant. Equations (\ref{eqn:inplane}) are inverted exactly by:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Compute $h = rv_\theta$, then solve $h^2 = a^4\Omega_a^2$ for the semimajor axis,
    $a = R_p\left(C + \sqrt{C^2 - 3J_2/2}\right)$ where $C = h^2/2GM_pR_p$.
\item Compute $\Omega_a$ and $\kappa_a$ from Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:Omega_kappa}).
\item Compute $e\sin M = (v_r/a\kappa_a)(a/r)^3$ and $e\cos M = \frac{1}{2}[(a/r)^2 - 1]$,
    which give $e$ and $M$.
\item Compute $\tilde{\omega} = \theta - (\Omega_a/\kappa_a)(M + 2e\sin M)$.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Relation to epi\_int and epi\_int\_lite}
\label{sec:appendix_wire2d}

%equations G3, G6, G10, G14 of notes/sympy/streamline_gravity.py; epi_int_lite issues 7 and 9
In the plane, the pull of Section \ref{sec:wire} reduces to Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}), the pull of
Fig.~3 of \cite{HS13} (Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_planar}). \cite{HS13} give its components as
$A_{g,r} = A_g\cos\varphi$ and $A_{g,\theta} = -A_g\sin\varphi$, with $A_g = 2G\lambda/\Delta$ and
$\Delta$ the perpendicular distance, and approximate them as $A_{g,r} \simeq A_g$ and
$A_{g,\theta} \simeq -A_gv_r'/v_\theta'$ (their Eqns.\ 20), to lowest order in the streamline's
eccentricity. With $\Delta$ read as the radial gap $d_r$ and $\tan\varphi = v_r'/v_\theta'$, those
are Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) without approximation. Epi\_int\_lite \citep{H25} differs from
Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) in three ways, which epi\_int\_3d corrects.

\paragraph{Distance} Epi\_int\_lite evaluates the pull's magnitude at the crossing point,
$\Delta = d_r$, but keeps its direction toward the near point,
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:wire_lite}
    A_{g,r} &=& \frac{2G\lambda\cos\varphi}{d_r} \nonumber\\
    A_{g,\theta} &=& -\frac{2G\lambda\sin\varphi}{d_r}.
\end{eqnarray}
This pull is weaker than Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d}) by the factor $\cos\varphi = 1 -
{\cal O}(\varphi^2)$. In a planetary ring $\varphi$ is of order the streamlines' eccentricities, so
the difference is $\sim\!10^{-10}$ and of no practical consequence, but Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_2d})
cost no more.

\paragraph{Direction} Epi\_int\_lite takes the wire's direction from the streamline's velocity at
the crossing point, $\sin\varphi = v_r'/v'$ and $\cos\varphi = v_\theta'/v'$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wire_velocity}). The velocity is tangent to the streamline only when the
streamline's shape is stationary. If the shape is a pattern that rotates at $\Omega_p$ while its
particles orbit at $\Omega$, then $v_r'/v_\theta' = (1 - \Omega_p/\Omega)s_r$, where $s_r$ is the
slope of the streamline's shape (Eqn.\ \ref{eqn:wire_slopes}). Near an $m$-armed Lindblad resonance
$1 - \Omega_p/\Omega \simeq \pm1/m$, so the velocity underestimates the wire's tilt, and with it the
tangential pull that transports angular momentum between streamlines, by a factor of about $m$. For
the slowly precessing $m = 1$ ringlets of \cite{H25}, $\Omega_p$ is the precession rate and the error
is negligible. Epi\_int\_3d takes the direction from the shape (Eqn.\ \ref{eqn:wire_t}).

\paragraph{Linear density} Epi\_int\_lite evaluates every perturbing streamline's pull with the
perturbed particle's own linear density $\lambda$, rather than the perturbing streamline's
$\lambda'$. The difference, $\lambda' - \lambda \simeq (a' - a)\partial\lambda/\partial a$, adds a
nearly uniform pull of order the ring's mass over the planet's; in the eccentric ringlets of
\cite{H25} it changes the precession rate by about $10^{-7}$ of itself.

In an eccentric ringlet the three corrections together change the streamlines' eccentricities by less
than $10^{-4}$ of themselves over 5000 orbits. Only the second matters for spiral waves, where it
changes the torque between streamlines by a factor of about $m$. Epi\_int\_3d keeps
epi\_int\_lite's ring gravity, Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:wire_lite}), as an option, so that its results can be
checked against epi\_int\_lite's exactly.

\section{Resolution limit of the streamline method}
\label{sec:appendix_zigzag}

%draft (2026-10-18); equations are sympy-checked P6, P7 in notes/sympy/ring_pressure.py
A streamline model represents a continuous ring by $N_r$ streamlines spaced $\Delta$ apart, and
that discreteness sets which wavelengths of the ring's response it can follow. This appendix
derives the axisymmetric stability of a uniform lattice of circular streamlines under the
self-gravity and pressure accelerations of epi\_int\_lite \citep{HS13, H25}, and compares it
with the continuum Toomre criterion.

Each streamline of linear density $\lambda$ exerts the acceleration $2G\lambda/d$ of a straight
wire on a particle a distance $d$ away \citep{HS13}. The pressure $p = c^2\sigma$ acts through
the surface density $\sigma_j = 2\lambda/(r_{j+1} - r_{j-1})$ of streamline $j$ and the central
difference $A_j = -(p_{j+1} - p_{j-1})/2\lambda$, which tends to $-\sigma^{-1}\partial p/\partial r$
as $\Delta \rightarrow 0$. Displace the streamlines radially by $\xi_j = \xi\cos(j\phi)$, where
$\phi = k\Delta$ and $k$ is the radial wavenumber, so that $0 < \phi \le \pi$; $\phi = \pi$ is
the shortest wave the lattice can carry, the zigzag $\xi_j = (-1)^j\xi$ of
Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}a. Linearizing in $\xi$, the streamlines at $j = \pm n$ add
$4G\lambda(1 - \cos n\phi)\xi/(n\Delta)^2$ to the acceleration of streamline 0, and summing over
$n$ with $\sigma = \lambda/\Delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_X}
    X(\phi) = \frac{4G\sigma}{\Delta}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1 - \cos n\phi}{n^2}
            = \frac{4G\sigma}{\Delta}\left(\frac{\pi\phi}{2} - \frac{\phi^2}{4}\right),
\end{equation}
while pressure contributes
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_p}
    \omega_p^2(\phi) = \left(\frac{c}{\Delta}\right)^2\sin^2\phi,
\end{equation}
so that each mode oscillates at the frequency
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_disp}
    \omega^2 = \kappa^2 - X(\phi) + \omega_p^2(\phi).
\end{equation}
For $\phi \ll 1$ these recover the continuum terms $X \rightarrow 2\pi G\sigma k$ and
$\omega_p^2 \rightarrow c^2k^2$, and Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_disp}) becomes the dispersion relation
of a pressure-supported self-gravitating sheet, which is stable at all $k$ when
$Q = c\kappa/\pi G\sigma \ge 1$ \citep{T64, BT87}. At the grid scale the lattice departs from
the continuum in two ways (Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}b). Self-gravity peaks at
$X(\pi) = \pi^2G\sigma/\Delta$, half the continuum value $2\pi G\sigma k$ at $k = \pi/\Delta$.
And pressure vanishes there: in the zigzag, streamlines $j \pm 1$ move together, so
$r_{j+1} - r_{j-1}$ and hence $\sigma_j$ and $p_j$ do not change. The viscous fluxes of
\cite{HS13}, which are differenced in the same way, likewise exert no force on the zigzag.

A cold continuum disk ($c = 0$) is unstable to all wavelengths shorter than
$\lambda_{\rm crit} = 4\pi^2G\sigma/\kappa^2$, and the lattice hides these: by
Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_X}) a cold lattice is axisymmetrically stable as long as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:zigzag_R}
    {\cal R} \equiv \frac{\lambda_{\rm crit}}{2\Delta} = \frac{2\pi^2G\sigma}{\kappa^2\Delta}
    \simeq \frac{\pi G m_r N_r}{\kappa^2 a\Delta a^2} < 2,
\end{equation}
where $2\Delta$ is the shortest resolved wavelength, and the last form is for a ringlet of mass
$m_r$, semimajor axis $a$ and width $\Delta a$, with $\sigma \simeq m_r/2\pi a\Delta a$ and
$\Delta \simeq \Delta a/N_r$. Adding streamlines to a given ringlet raises ${\cal R}$ in
proportion to $N_r$.

%O1-O5, sympy-checked in notes/sympy/viscous_overstability.py
A viscous ringlet must also avoid the viscous overstability, the growth of axisymmetric epicyclic
oscillations that viscous stresses drive when the shear stress rises steeply enough with surface
density \citep{ST95, LR95, SSS01, S01, LO06, LO08}. For a cold ringlet with constant kinematic shear
and bulk viscosities $\nu_s$ and $\nu_b$, as in epi\_int\_lite, and Keplerian $\kappa = \Omega$, a
mode with oscillation frequency $\omega \simeq \kappa(1 - x)^{1/2}$ grows to first order in the
viscosities at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:overstability}
    \gamma = \frac{K}{6(1 - x)}\left[\nu_s(4x + 2) - 3\nu_b(1 - x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $K = k^2$ and $x = 2\pi G\sigma k/\kappa^2$ in the continuum. Without self-gravity ($x = 0$)
the ringlet is stable when $\nu_b > 2\nu_s/3$, the onset found by \cite{ST95} and \cite{LSS17} for a
viscosity independent of $\sigma$, while self-gravity is destabilizing \citep{LSS17}: a mode is
stable only for $x < (3\nu_b - 2\nu_s)/(4\nu_s + 3\nu_b)$, which is $1/7$ for $\nu_b = \nu_s$ and
$0.4$ for $\nu_b = 2\nu_s$. The streamline lattice follows Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:overstability}) with the
grid's versions of both terms: $K = \sin^2\phi/\Delta^2$, from the code's central differences of the
viscous stresses, and $x = X(\phi)/\kappa^2$. Both stresses vanish at the zigzag, $\phi = \pi$, where
self-gravity is strongest, $X(\pi)/\kappa^2 = {\cal R}/2$; so a viscous lattice is stable at every
wavelength when ${\cal R} < 2/7$ for $\nu_b = \nu_s$, or ${\cal R} < 4/5$ for $\nu_b = 2\nu_s$, more
stringent than Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}). Since $K\nu \sim \nu/\Delta^2$, an overstable lattice's
modes grow faster the more streamlines it has.

That limit matters because a ringlet's self-gravity must be strong enough to resist the
differential precession due to $J_2$. Across a ringlet of width $\Delta a$, $J_2$ drives a
differential precession $\propto J_2(R_p/a)^2\Omega_a\Delta a/a$, while for a small nonlinearity
parameter the ringlet's self-gravity drives one $\propto (m_r/M_p)\Omega_a\Delta\tilde{\omega}(a/\Delta a)^2$,
where $\Delta\tilde{\omega}$ is the periapse twist across it (cf.\ Eqn.\ C1 of \citealt{H25}).
%TODO: this scaling is an order-of-magnitude estimate that fits the runs below; derive it properly
So keeping $J_2$ from winding up the ringlet requires $m_r \propto J_2\Delta a^3/\Delta\tilde{\omega}$,
and then ${\cal R} \propto J_2\Delta a N_r/\Delta\tilde{\omega}$: a wider ringlet does not help, and
for a given planet more streamlines can be afforded only by a narrower ringlet. This is tested
here with simulations of a ringlet of width $\Delta a = 0.0125a$ whose edges are tapered, mass $m_r = 3\times10^{-8}M_p$,
eccentricity $e = 10^{-4}$, periapses twisted by $\pi$ from its inner to outer edge, and kinematic
viscosity $\nu = 7\times10^{-11}a^2\Omega_a$, with no pressure, $R_p = 0.5a$ and $J_2 = 0.01$. With
$N_r = 100$ streamlines (${\cal R} \simeq 0.09$ in the ringlet's middle, where the taper makes
$\sigma$ 1.25 times and $\Delta$ 0.8 times their ringlet-wide means) the ringlet stays smooth for $10^4$ orbits while
self-gravity, rather than $J_2$, controls its shape: within about 1000 orbits it
straightens the initial twist, and the ringlet's body then stays nearly aligned, as two sub-ringlets that precess slowly relative to each other. With $N_r = 300$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.3$) and
$N_r = 1000$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.9$) it becomes ragged and its streamlines cross after about
190 and 30 orbits. At $N_r = 1000$ the disturbance starts in the ringlet's interior, grows at the
same rate with 40 or 241 particles per streamline, and does not grow when ring gravity is turned
off, consistent with the self-gravity-assisted overstability for $\nu_b = \nu_s$ at
${\cal R} > 2/7$. Pressure with $Q \le 2$ delays the crossing by less than two orbits, as expected
from Fig.~\ref{fig:zigzag}b, where $\omega_p^2 \le 0.1\kappa^2$.

The crossings near ${\cal R} \simeq 0.3$ are mostly a different, numerical, problem. The same
ringlet without the initial twist, at $N_r = 333$ (${\cal R} \simeq 0.31$), crosses after 145
orbits, and its code's own linearized axisymmetric modes, computed from the reference implementation's
accelerations, grow by only $3.3\times10^{-3}$ per orbit, far too slowly. The disturbance that crosses is instead a sawtooth
along each streamline with azimuthal wavenumber $m \simeq N_t/2$, where $N_t$ is the number of
particles per streamline. It also grows in a circular ringlet, without ring gravity, and without
viscosity, though more slowly (after about 220, 170 and 850 orbits), its onset varies
non-monotonically with $N_t$ (with $N_t = 30$ or 50 the ringlet survives $10^4$ orbits, with 20, 40
or 320 it does not), and pressure with $Q \le 10$ delays it by at most 20\%. It is presumably seeded by
the 3-point Lagrange interpolation in longitude that couples neighboring streamlines' particles,
and it remains an open problem.

So Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:zigzag_R}) is a necessary condition only. In practice a simulated ringlet whose
self-gravity matters should be run with ${\cal R} \lesssim 0.1$, viscous or not, and a ringlet that
needs more streamlines must be made narrower and much less massive.
