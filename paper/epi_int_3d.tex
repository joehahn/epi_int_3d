\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

%placeholder
The epi\_int and epi\_int\_lite N-body integrators \citep{HS13, H25} model a planetary ring as
a set of gravitating streamlines whose particles are confined to the ring plane. Spiral bending
waves, which are launched at a satellite's vertical resonances \citep{SCL83, S84}, require the
particles' vertical motions too. This paper extends epi\_int\_lite into the vertical direction
and uses the new code, epi\_int\_3d, to simulate spiral bending waves.


\section{Vertical epicyclic motion}
\label{sec:vertical}

The epi\_int\_lite drift step advances a particle's unperturbed orbit about an oblate planet
using first-order epicyclic equations (Eqns.\ A9--A10 of \citealt{H25}) that are exactly
reversible: converting spatial coordinates into epicyclic orbit elements and back introduces no
error, so the drift step is not a source of numerical drift during simulations lasting
$10^5$--$10^6$ orbits. Here those equations are extended to particles having vertical
displacement $z$ and vertical velocity $v_z$, with the same first-order accuracy and the same
exact reversibility.

The planet's potential, extended to three dimensions, is the $J_2$ term of the zonal-harmonic
expansion in Eqn.\ (5) of \cite{BL94},
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:Phi3d}
    \Phi(r,z) = -\frac{GM_p}{\rho}\left[1 - J_2\left(\frac{R}{\rho}\right)^2P_2\left(\frac{z}{\rho}\right)\right]
\end{equation}
where $M_p$ is the planet's mass, $R$ its radius, $\rho^2 = r^2 + z^2$, $r$ is the
cylindrical radius, and $P_2(\mu) = (3\mu^2 - 1)/2$. In the ring plane this reduces to
Eqn.\ (A2) of \cite{H25}, so the mean orbital frequency $\Omega(a)$ and epicyclic frequency
$\kappa(a)$ are unchanged (Eqns.\ A7--A8 of \citealt{H25}). Expanding $\Phi$ to second order in
$z$ gives the vertical frequency $\nu^2 = \partial^2\Phi/\partial z^2$ at $z=0$
(Eqn.\ 17 of \citealt{BL94}):
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:nu}
    \nu(a) = \sqrt{\frac{GM_p}{a^3}}\left[1 + \frac{9}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R}{a}\right)^2\right]^{1/2},
\end{equation}
which satisfies $\kappa^2 + \nu^2 = 2\Omega^2$ as required by Laplace's equation.

\subsection{Elements to coordinates}
\label{sec:elem2coords}

Two orbit elements are added: the inclination $i$ and the longitude of ascending node
$\hat{\Omega}$. The in-plane coordinates $r, \theta, v_r, v_\theta$ are given by
Eqns.\ (A9) of \cite{H25} without change, and the vertical coordinates are
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:zvz}
    z   &=& a\,i\sin(\lambda - \hat{\Omega}) \nonumber\\
    v_z &=& a\nu i\cos(\lambda - \hat{\Omega})
\end{eqnarray}
where $\lambda = \tilde{\omega} + (\Omega/\kappa)M$ is the particle's mean longitude,
{\it i.e.}\ $\theta$ without its $2e\sin M$ term. The node regresses at the rate
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:node}
    \dot{\hat{\Omega}} = \Omega - \nu \simeq -\frac{3}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R}{a}\right)^2\Omega,
\end{equation}
so the vertical phase $\lambda - \hat{\Omega}$ advances at exactly $\nu$, and
Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) are an exact solution of $\ddot{z} = -\nu^2z$. Taking the vertical phase
from $\lambda$ rather than from $\theta$ is what keeps the vertical motion a pure harmonic
oscillator; using $\theta - \hat{\Omega}$ would add an ${\cal O}(ei)$ error to the phase.
The original epi\_int code used the vertical phase $\tilde{\omega} + M - \hat{\Omega}$,
which agrees with ours to first order.

\subsection{Coordinates to elements}
\label{sec:coords2elem}

The inversion is steps 1--4 of \cite{H25} Appendix A, followed by a new step 5.
Step 1 remains exact in three dimensions: $h = rv_\theta$ is the $z$-component of the
particle's specific angular momentum, which is conserved in the axisymmetric potential
(\ref{eqn:Phi3d}), so Eqn.\ (A10) of \cite{H25} still yields $a$ exactly when $r$ is the
cylindrical radius. Steps 2--4 then provide $\Omega$, $\kappa$, $e$, $M$ and $\tilde{\omega}$,
and so $\lambda$. Step 5 computes $\nu(a)$ from Eqn.\ (\ref{eqn:nu}) and then
\begin{eqnarray}
    \label{eqn:i_node}
    i &=& \sqrt{\left(\frac{z}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{v_z}{a\nu}\right)^2} \nonumber\\
    \hat{\Omega} &=& \lambda - \text{atan2}(z, v_z/\nu).
\end{eqnarray}
Once $a$ is known the mapping $(i, \lambda - \hat{\Omega}) \leftrightarrow (z, v_z/\nu)$ is a
change between polar and Cartesian coordinates, so Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) and
(\ref{eqn:i_node}) are exact inverses. During the drift step from $t$ to $t + \Delta t$,
steps 1--5 convert coordinates to elements, the elements are advanced via
$M \rightarrow M + \kappa\Delta t$ and $\hat{\Omega} \rightarrow \hat{\Omega} + (\Omega - \nu)\Delta t$,
and Eqns.\ (A9) and (\ref{eqn:zvz}) convert back to coordinates. In the code the vertical part
of the drift is simply a rotation of $(z, v_z/\nu)$ through the angle $\nu\Delta t$.

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:accuracy}

Inserting $r = a(1 + \delta)$ with $\delta = {\cal O}(e)$ into the exact vertical equation of
motion $\ddot{z} = -\partial\Phi/\partial z$ shows that Eqns.\ (\ref{eqn:zvz}) satisfy it with
a residual of order ${\cal O}(ei)$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eqn:residual}
    \ddot{z} + \nu^2z \simeq \frac{3GM_p}{a^3}\left[1 + \frac{15}{2}J_2\left(\frac{R}{a}\right)^2\right]\delta z,
\end{equation}
plus terms of order ${\cal O}(i^3)$. Likewise the in-plane Eqns.\ (A9) ignore the ${\cal O}(i^2)$
change in the radial force on a particle at height $z$. The solution is therefore accurate
to first order, with errors of order ${\cal O}(e^2, i^2, ei)$, the same order as
Eqns.\ (A9) alone. These equations, and the inversion (\ref{eqn:i_node}), were verified
symbolically.

Table \ref{tab:accuracy} confirms this numerically for Saturn-like $J_2 = 0.016$ and $a = 2R$.
Converting $10^5$ random particles having $e, i \le 0.05$ from coordinates to elements and
back reproduces the coordinates with relative errors $\lesssim 10^{-15}$, and $10^4$ repeated
round trips change $r$ by $\lesssim 2\times10^{-15}a$ and $z$ by $\lesssim 2\times10^{-13}a$.
After one orbit, the position error relative to an exact integration of the particle's motion
in potential (\ref{eqn:Phi3d}) falls as the square of $e$ and $i$.

\cite{BL94} and \cite{RS06} give conversions between coordinates and geometric
elements for inclined orbits about an oblate planet that are accurate to second order in $e$
and $i$. But those conversions are not exact inverses of each other (\citealt{H25};
the inversion of \citealt{RS06} is iterative and accurate to second order), so the repeated
conversions made during a long simulation accumulate error. The first-order equations given here trade that higher accuracy
for exact reversibility, as \cite{H25} did for the in-plane motion.
